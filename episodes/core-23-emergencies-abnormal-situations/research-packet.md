# Emergencies and Abnormal Situations — research packet

## Scope

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners, using a typical single-engine land trainer in daytime VFR.
- Target duration: approximately 25–30 minutes, subject to actual spoken word count and later render.
- Core question: As a problem develops, how does the pilot preserve control, choose a reachable landing option, use the airplane's procedure, and obtain help early?
- Out of scope: aircraft-specific speeds and switch sequences; detailed systems troubleshooting; a practiced turn-back technique after takeoff; instrument-flight emergency procedures; multiengine tasks E–G; seaplane ditching technique; detailed survival and rescue procedure.
- Scenario: Elena and Jonah fly the familiar trainer from Episodes 21–22 on a daytime VFR trip over farmland. Elena has identified practical airports and landing terrain before departure, knows where the airplane's checklist and emergency equipment are, and briefs Jonah. En route, engine roughness and an abnormal indication prompt a precautionary diversion. The lesson then asks what changes if power is lost before that airport is reached. A separate examiner-style case tests an alternator failure; another tests smoke and emergency descent. These are branches, not sequential disasters on the same flight.

## Source classification and bounds

- ACS tasks describe test outcomes, not a universal engine-failure checklist.
- The AFH provides generic guidance and explicitly defers to the manufacturer. The script avoids naming a best-glide number, a switch position, a fire procedure, or a flap setting for this fictional trainer.
- AIM communication and assistance guidance is distinct from the legal emergency authority in 14 CFR 91.3. The script does not imply that declaring an emergency alone automatically authorizes any deviation.
- Fictional engine behavior, fields, airports and passenger actions are teaching synthesis, not reports about a real aircraft.
- A precautionary landing while power remains and a forced landing after power loss are different conditions. The story makes the change explicit.
- The separate electrical and smoke cases are alternatives. A generator failure does not automatically imply engine failure in all airplanes; equipment dependencies are model-specific.
- AFH Chapter 18 calls a turn-back after low-altitude takeoff a model- and training-specific choice with substantial risk. This episode does not teach it as a general procedure; low-altitude turn-back training belongs with a CFI in the actual airplane.

## Likely trouble spots

- Treating “aviate, navigate, communicate” as a slogan with no observable priorities. The episode repeatedly states what Elena controls, what landing option she chooses, and what she communicates.
- Continuing past a suitable airport to diagnose a deteriorating engine. The AFH precautionary-landing distinction gives the decision its timing.
- Trying to stretch a glide with pitch instead of comparing available altitude, wind and reachable terrain at the airplane's recommended best glide speed.
- Letting checklists, radio or passenger tasks interrupt airplane control and the landing path.
- Treating a radio phrase or squawk as a substitute for a plain report of the problem, location and intended action.
- Presenting emergency authority as permission to deviate for convenience, or reporting as automatic rather than upon FAA request.
- Treating an ELT as guaranteed to activate or as a substitute for radio and route information.
- Describing an emergency descent as simply diving faster, despite structural and configuration speed limits.

## Open technical questions

- None for this general lesson. Aircraft-specific numbers and sequences remain intentionally outside the script and must be taken from the airplane's current AFM/POH during actual training and flight.
