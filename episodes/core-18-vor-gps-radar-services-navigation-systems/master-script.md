**LEARNER:**

Where does DME fit while I am preparing the VOR?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

DME means Distance Measuring Equipment. In a VOR/DME or VORTAC installation, the VOR answers the direction question and DME answers the distance question for the same named facility. The DME receiver sends a ranging question to the ground station and times the reply; the resulting display is the straight-line distance between the airplane and that facility.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-dme]

Picture the station as a point on the ground. A chart measures a route across the ground’s surface. DME measures the direct line from your airplane to that point. These two numbers will be different because your airplane is at altitude when in flight. Far away from the DME station, the difference between the chart distance and the DME distance may be small. Close to the station, altitude becomes obvious: an airplane directly above a station at 6,000 feet has roughly one nautical mile of DME distance even though its horizontal chart distance is zero. That direct-line measurement is called slant range.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-dme]

The preflight questions are specific: does the planned facility have DME, and what distance reference will it provide along this route? The pairing is direct. The VOR supplies direction from the station, and DME supplies distance to the station. Together they describe the airplane’s position relative to the same facility while keeping those two jobs distinct.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-basics]

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-dme]

The pair becomes easier to reconstruct when you give the station a fixed place in your mental picture. “Harbor, on the one-eight-zero radial, ten DME” means Harbor VOR is north of the airplane, the airplane lies on the station’s south radial, and the airplane is ten nautical miles away by the direct airborne distance. The selected course needed for the next leg is a separate choice. That three-part picture—facility, radial, and DME distance—gives the pilot a way to describe position before looking at a moving map.

## [10:00] VOR and DME: build the navigation reference

**ANNOUNCER:**

VOR and DME: build the navigation reference.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

With the Harbor VOR tuned and identified, we can give its information a physical picture. A VOR is a VHF ground station that sends magnetic courses called radials outward in every direction, like spokes from a hub. A radial is named for the magnetic bearing away from the station. The one-eight-zero radial extends south from Harbor VOR. The course that reaches the station from that southern line is the reciprocal: three-six-zero inbound.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-basics]

VOR reception is line of sight. The altitude of the receiving airplane and the distance from the facility affect usable range. The flight plan therefore treats a VOR as a reference with a coverage question, and the in-flight identification and signal indication answer whether the selected receiver is supplying that reference at that moment.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-basics]

At a lower altitude, terrain or the earth’s curvature can put the receiver outside the VOR’s usable line of sight sooner than the route distance alone would suggest. At a higher altitude, the signal can reach farther. That is why the source check has both a planning side and an in-flight side: plan with the facility’s expected service volume, then identify the station and read the receiver’s status before using the indication.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-basics]

**LEARNER:**

I have Harbor identified, and I understand that the one-eight-zero radial is south of the station. How does the CDI turn that geometry into an indication?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

First select the line you intend to use. For our northbound leg to Harbor VOR, select three-six-zero, the inbound reciprocal of the one-eight-zero radial. The course selector is the question you give the indicator: “Where is the airplane relative to this three-six-zero line through Harbor VOR?” The CDI needle answers with the line’s location relative to the airplane. A left deflection places the selected course to the left of the airplane; a right deflection places it to the right. The TO/FROM indication adds the direction sense: whether following the selected course leads toward or away from the station.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-indicator]

A horizontal situation indicator, or HSI, presents the same course relationship on a display that also shows the airplane’s heading. Think of it as putting the heading picture and the selected navigation course onto one instrument. The heading marker shows where the airplane is pointed. The course arrow shows the course the pilot selected for that VOR. The course-deviation bar shows whether that selected course lies left or right of the airplane. The same VOR geometry governs both displays, while the HSI makes the heading and selected-course comparison easier to see together.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-indicator]

For any leg that approaches a VOR from the south, three-six-zero with a TO indication describes the inbound reference. A left-of-center CDI means the selected inbound line lies left of the airplane, placing the airplane right of that three-six-zero line. The pilot can compare that result with the charted route, current heading, DME distance when available, GPS, and the outside landmarks before choosing an adjustment.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-indicator]

The important habit is to name the complete relationship: selected course, needle side, TO or FROM, and the airplane’s position relative to the selected line. That statement makes the comparison with the route picture much easier to follow. A centered needle with the expected TO or FROM sense becomes one well-defined contribution to the position check after the station is identified and the receiver shows a usable signal.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-indicator]

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-integrity]

As the airplane reaches Harbor VOR, the PHAK describes the CDI needle fluctuating and then settling while the TO indication changes to FROM. The event makes sense in the ground picture: the airplane has passed from the approaching side of the station to the departing side. With the facility, selected course, expected DME distance, and route beyond the station considered beforehand, the pilot can recognize that change as the planned passage event.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-vor-station-passage]

**LEARNER:**

Are VOR and DME both fading away as GPS becomes more common?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

They have different roles in the FAA’s current navigation architecture. The FAA is reducing the larger CONUS VOR network to the VOR Minimum Operational Network, or VOR MON. The remaining network is a conventional backup structure for a GPS outage: it is intended to support VOR station-to-station navigation or a route to a MON airport. The planned coverage is built around larger service volumes beginning at 5,000 feet above ground level, so the specific altitude and facility information remain part of preflight planning.

[Source: sources.yaml#faa-vor-mon]

DME has an additional role in that architecture. The AIM distinguishes aircraft equipped for DME/DME RNAV from aircraft that cannot continue RNAV during a GNSS disruption. Suitably equipped aircraft can use DME/DME RNAV to continue performance-based navigation. For aircraft without that capability, the VOR MON provides the broad conventional navigation fallback.

[Source: sources.yaml#aim-vor-mon-dme-dme]

[Source: sources.yaml#faa-vor-mon]

DME/DME RNAV is separate from reading distance to one facility on a standalone DME display. A scanning DME receiver uses range information from multiple high-power DME facilities to determine position. The FAA says this supports RNAV en-route instrument flight procedures, and its NextGen DME program sustains the high-power DME facilities required for those procedures as a resilient GPS backup.

[Source: sources.yaml#faa-nextgen-dme]

[Source: sources.yaml#faa-ground-based-navigation]

For this general-aviation VFR lesson, that is architecture context rather than a procedure to fly. It explains why the FAA continues to retain and modernize DME while VOR MON serves conventional navigation. The equipment in the actual airplane determines whether DME means a distance readout from one facility or a suitably equipped RNAV system using multiple facilities.

GPS: a prepared position source.

A receiver with RAIM can alert for insufficient satellites for integrity monitoring or a detected potential error beyond the applicable limit. The approved information for a particular receiver tells the pilot how its status and alerts are presented.

**LEARNER:**

Where does TIS-B fit into what I see on an ADS-B In display?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

ADS-B In traffic can arrive directly from another ADS-B-equipped aircraft. It can also arrive through ground-based services. Traffic Information Service–Broadcast, or TIS-B, is the ground-based layer that adds surveillance information about certain nearby transponder-equipped aircraft that are not broadcasting ADS-B Out. That is why it belongs in an ADS-B In traffic discussion: it can add target information to the traffic picture received in the cockpit.

[Source: sources.yaml#faa-adsb-in-services]

TIS-B is a client-based service. The FAA describes the client as an aircraft equipped with ADS-B Out and ADS-B In that meets the ground system’s conditions. TIS-B targets must be transponder equipped and within radar coverage. An ADS-B In-only receiver can receive proximate air-to-air ADS-B traffic, while the ground system uses a qualifying client aircraft’s ADS-B Out position to tailor TIS-B and ADS-R traffic services in the surrounding area.

[Source: sources.yaml#faa-adsb-in-services]

The practical equipment question is therefore: what traffic sources does this installed or portable receiver display under the conditions of this flight? Many current VFR installations pair an ADS-B In receiver with an electronic flight-bag display, but the answer varies with the installed transmitter, receiver links, antennas, app version, and ground coverage. For a Sentry receiver and ForeFlight display, the manufacturer’s current Sentry Pilot’s Guide is a useful equipment-specific reference; its traffic-source indications and limitations belong in that product documentation rather than in a general lesson.

[Source: sources.yaml#foreflight-sentry-pilot-guide]

On a Sentry/ForeFlight setup, the guide explains that a traffic detail can identify whether a target arrived through direct ADS-B or TIS-B. The label documents how that receiver and display identify the source of the traffic information.

What does the flight-following relationship add during the flight?

I establish radio contact with the radar facility and request VFR radar traffic advisories. As ATC establishes radar contact, it may assign a computer-generated beacon code, and I operate the transponder on that ATC-specified Mode 3/A code with altitude reporting enabled. A requested code change can help ATC establish radar identification. Once ATC has radar identification and can provide the service, the advisory relationship begins. The service can provide traffic information and other advisory help when coverage and workload permit. I remain responsible for the VFR flight, monitor the assigned frequency, respond when able, keep looking outside, and communicate constraints or a request for further assistance.
