## [06:55] Read the route as a physical picture

**ANNOUNCER:**

Read the route as a physical picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Before drawing attention to small symbols, look at the broad shape of the route. In the Chart Users’ Guide, the terrain system includes contour lines, shaded relief, color tints, obstruction symbols, and Maximum Elevation Figures. Contour lines join equal elevations. Their pattern and spacing provide a visual sense of terrain: widely spaced contours represent gentler slopes, while closely spaced contours represent steeper slopes.

[Source: sources.yaml#chart-guide-terrain]
[Claim type: FAA chart guidance]

On our fictional route, the western third crosses widely spaced contours and lighter terrain tint. Farther east, the contours close together and the shaded relief becomes more pronounced. Those details give us a planning question before they give us an answer: does the direct line enter terrain that deserves a closer look, and would another route offer a clearer outside picture or more options?

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

The Cross-Country Flight Planning task expects altitude selection to account for terrain and obstacles, glide distance, VFR cruising altitudes, and wind. Begin by identifying the terrain and obstacles that the route must clear and the altitudes that preserve useful glide options. Apply the VFR cruising-altitude rules, then compare the forecast winds aloft at the practical altitudes that remain. A more favorable wind can improve groundspeed and reduce time and fuel used, while a stronger headwind can do the opposite. **LEARNER:**

What should I do with an obstruction symbol near the line?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

First, read what the symbol actually says. [Source: sources.yaml#chart-guide-obstacles]

If the chart shows a tower top at two thousand seven hundred feet MSL with one thousand feet in parentheses, the obstacle rises one thousand feet above its local ground and reaches two thousand seven hundred feet on the MSL elevation scale. Those numbers answer different questions, so preserve their labels.

[Source: sources.yaml#chart-guide-obstacles]
[Claim type: FAA chart guidance and teaching synthesis]

**ANNOUNCER:**

Choose checkpoints that can survive first contact.

Pilotage is navigation by reference to landmarks or checkpoints. A checkpoint is a feature chosen to help confirm position and progress. The PHAK recommends prominent features common to the area, points that can be identified by other nearby features, route brackets such as highways, rivers, railroads, or mountains, and enough checkpoints that the flight does not depend completely on one.

## [19:20] Build the dead-reckoning picture

**ANNOUNCER:**

Build the dead-reckoning picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Dead reckoning is navigation by computations based on time, airspeed, distance, and direction. After adjustment for wind, those variables produce a predicted heading, meaning the direction the airplane’s nose should point, and groundspeed, meaning the rate of progress over the ground. The heading is intended to take the airplane along the planned path, and the groundspeed establishes predicted time to each checkpoint and the destination. The PHAK says dead reckoning is usually used with pilotage for ordinary cross-country flight, so the predictions are monitored and corrected from observed checkpoints.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-dead-reckoning]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

**LEARNER:**

Before the calculation, can we separate course, heading, and track? They sound like three names for direction.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Course is the intended path over the ground, or the direction of the line drawn on the chart. Heading is the direction the airplane’s nose points. Track is the actual path the airplane makes over the ground. When wind correction works as planned, track and course coincide even though heading may point to one side of that course.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-navigation-terms]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

Picture a moving walkway at an angle. The airplane moves through the air mass while the air mass moves over the ground. Airspeed describes the airplane’s progress through the air. Groundspeed describes its progress over the ground. A headwind can reduce groundspeed without reducing the airplane’s speed through the surrounding air, and a tailwind can increase groundspeed. A crosswind can move the track left or right of the heading.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-wind-track]
[Source: sources.yaml#phak-navigation-terms]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance and teaching analogy]

The wind correction angle is the angular correction applied to the course to establish a heading that offsets drift. If the wind tends to move the airplane right of course, the planned heading points left of course by the calculated amount. That is why a correct heading can look different from the direction of the route line.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-wind-track]
[Source: sources.yaml#phak-navigation-terms]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

**LEARNER:**

And true airspeed is the airspeed value used in that wind calculation?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. True airspeed is the airplane’s speed through the air used for this wind calculation. For our fictional planning example, the expected true airspeed is one hundred five knots. The fictional wind is from three three zero degrees at fifteen knots, and the charted true course is zero seven five degrees. A wind-triangle calculation gives a wind correction of about eight degrees left, a true heading of about zero six seven degrees, and a groundspeed of about one hundred eight knots.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-wind-track]
[Source: sources.yaml#phak-course-computation]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance and teaching synthesis]

Notice what each result answers. The true course says where the route line points relative to true north. The true heading says where the nose should point relative to true north after the forecast wind correction. The groundspeed predicts how quickly the airplane should move along the ground if the assumptions hold.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-navigation-terms]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance and teaching synthesis]

Remember that these calculations are estimates built from forecast wind, expected aircraft performance, planned timing, and correctly identified checkpoints. Actual wind can differ from the forecast. The airplane may not produce the planned true airspeed. Climb and leveling can affect early timing, and a checkpoint can be identified late or timed from the wrong moment. Build the plan with room for those differences, then monitor what actually happens. Pilotage supplies the observed position; dead reckoning supplies the predicted position. Their agreement builds confidence, and their disagreement prompts a check before a conclusion.

## [23:20] Turn the course into a heading

**ANNOUNCER:**

Turn the course into a heading.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The route line and wind correction are referenced to true north, but a magnetic compass responds to magnetic direction and to magnetic influences in the airplane. Variation is the angle between true north and magnetic north. The sectional’s isogonic lines show variation for geographic locations so a true direction can be converted to a magnetic direction.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-variation-deviation]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

Deviation is different. It is the deflection caused by magnetic influences within a particular aircraft, and it can vary with heading. The aircraft’s deviation card provides the correction used to convert magnetic heading to the compass heading to steer.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-deviation-card]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

**LEARNER:**

So variation belongs to the geographic location, while deviation belongs to this airplane and this heading.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Remember the difference by connecting each correction to its source. Variation comes from the chart for the route. Deviation comes from the aircraft’s compass deviation card. They are not interchangeable corrections.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-variation-deviation]
[Source: sources.yaml#phak-deviation-card]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

Return to the numbers. We measured a true course of zero seven five. The forecast wind correction was eight degrees left, producing a true heading of zero six seven. The fictional isogonic line gives five degrees west variation. Applying that west variation produces a magnetic heading of zero seven two.

**ANNOUNCER:**

Build the navigation log.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-course-planning]

[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance]

A navigation log is the compact record of that plan. For our lesson, each row needs the checkpoint, leg distance, cumulative distance, planned compass heading, planned groundspeed, leg time, and cumulative time. Actual time and revised estimates have their own spaces so the observed flight does not overwrite the original prediction.

[Source: sources.yaml#phak-course-computation]
[Claim type: FAA handbook guidance and teaching synthesis]

Prepare that log on the ground as a workload-management tool. The plan also needs margin for actual conditions to differ from the forecast without making an immediate cockpit calculation urgent. **LEARNER:**

How do the distance and groundspeed become time?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Time equals distance divided by groundspeed. Distance equals groundspeed multiplied by time. Groundspeed equals distance divided by time. Because knots are nautical miles per hour, a twelve-nautical-mile leg at one hundred eight knots takes twelve divided by one hundred eight hours, or one ninth of an hour. Multiply by sixty and the result is six minutes forty seconds.

The fourth leg to the quarry and lake relationship is fourteen nautical miles and about seven minutes forty-seven seconds. The final twelve-mile leg is another six minutes forty seconds.

## [36:20] Keep the two methods working together

**ANNOUNCER:**

Keep the two methods working together.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Pilotage without a time-and-heading prediction can become a sequence of familiar-looking features with no disciplined progress check. Dead reckoning without outside confirmation can carry an unnoticed error forward. Used together, dead reckoning predicts where and when to look, while pilotage tests the prediction against the ground.

**LEARNER:**

Then a checkpoint has two jobs: confirm where I am and help test how the plan is performing.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. A confirmed checkpoint anchors position. Its actual crossing time, compared with the known leg distance, tests groundspeed and ETA. Its location relative to the course line tests the track. The planned compass heading records what the forecast said should work; the observed ground relationship shows what actually happened.

Define dead reckoning and its relationship to pilotage.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**LEARNER:**

Dead reckoning uses time, airspeed, distance, and direction, adjusted for wind, to predict heading, groundspeed, and arrival time. Pilotage compares those predictions with observed checkpoints. Separate course, heading, track, wind correction angle, airspeed, and groundspeed.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**LEARNER:**

Course is the intended ground path. Heading is where the airplane’s nose points. Track is the actual path over the ground. Wind correction angle adjusts course to obtain a heading that offsets drift. Airspeed is progress through the air, while groundspeed is progress over the ground.

Where do variation and deviation enter?

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**LEARNER:**

Variation is the charted angular difference between true north and magnetic north for the geographic area. Deviation is the aircraft- and heading-specific compass error shown by the airplane’s deviation card. After wind correction gives true heading, variation converts it to magnetic heading, and the card’s deviation correction gives the compass heading to steer.

Time equals distance divided by groundspeed. Distance equals groundspeed times time. Groundspeed equals distance divided by time. A confirmed twelve-nautical-mile leg completed in eight minutes gives an observed groundspeed of about ninety knots.
