## [03:20] Build a three-dimensional airspace picture

**ANNOUNCER:**

Build a three-dimensional airspace picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Open a sectional chart in your mind and place Meadow Creek near the center. A chart is flat, but airspace is a volume. Every airspace decision combines a horizontal position with an altitude. The lower boundary is the airspace floor. The upper boundary is its ceiling. A shelf is a layer that projects outward from a terminal area, leaving different airspace below it.

**LEARNER:**

We also use the word ceiling for weather.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes, and the two meanings must stay separate. An airspace ceiling is the top of an airspace volume. A weather ceiling describes a cloud or obscuration layer, and we will define that when weather enters the scenario.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The PHAK groups Classes Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, and Echo as controlled airspace. Class Golf is uncontrolled airspace: the portion not designated as one of those controlled classes. Controlled does not mean that every VFR airplane inside it has an individual clearance or is receiving separation. It means ATC service is provided according to that airspace classification. Class Echo is controlled airspace even though a VFR airplane normally has no specific communication requirement just to enter it.

**LEARNER:**

So “controlled” is a category, not a promise that a controller is watching my flight.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. Keep your collision avoidance, terrain clearance, weather, and navigation decisions active whether or not you are talking with ATC.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

At Meadow Creek, Class Golf begins at the surface. On our fictional chart, a magenta vignette surrounds the airport. The Chart Users’ Guide uses that shading to show a common Class Echo floor of 700 feet above ground level. Class Golf fills the space below that floor. If the airplane is 400 feet above the airport, it is in Class Golf. At 900 feet above the same airport, it is in Class Echo. Nothing changed on the map. The airplane crossed a vertical boundary.

**LEARNER:**

And that 700 feet is AGL, measured above the local surface, while my altimeter shows altitude in MSL.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. AGL means above ground level. MSL means above mean sea level. To know when the climb crosses that 700-foot AGL floor, the pilot relates airport or terrain elevation to the airplane’s MSL altitude. If Meadow Creek’s field elevation is 600 feet MSL, then a 700-foot AGL Class Echo floor is approximately 1,300 feet MSL over the airport.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Elsewhere, Class Echo may begin at 1,200 feet AGL, at the surface, or at a depicted MSL altitude. The PHAK explains that Class Golf extends from the surface to the base of the overlying Class Echo. The chart, not a blanket assumption, supplies the floor for the point being evaluated.

**LEARNER:**

This is where the chart colors become dangerous if I remember only “magenta means Echo.” I need to know which boundary or floor the symbol represents.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Dashed magenta identifies a Class Echo surface area. A magenta vignette commonly identifies Class Echo beginning at 700 feet AGL. A blue vignette can identify a 1,200-foot AGL floor, and other floors may be printed with an altitude. Dashed blue commonly outlines Class Delta. Solid blue outlines and altitude fractions depict Class Bravo. Solid magenta outlines and altitude fractions depict Class Charlie. Use the chart legend and the current Chart Users’ Guide, because the color is only one part of the symbol.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

PHAK Figure 15-1 is a useful visual aid for this three-dimensional picture, and it is linked in the show notes. The figure places terminal airspace, Class Echo, and Class Golf in one side view. Study it from the ground upward, then trace a sample climb across each boundary.

## [08:00] Match each class to its entry question

**ANNOUNCER:**

Match each class to its entry question.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The Meadow Creek climb reaches Class Echo without a radio call because Class Echo normally has no specific entry requirement for VFR flight. Far above this route, Class Alpha generally begins at 18,000 feet MSL and continues through Flight Level 600. Unless otherwise authorized, operations in Class Alpha are conducted under instrument flight rules, so it is not available as ordinary VFR cruise airspace.

**LEARNER:**

For our VFR flight, the classes that require an entry action are Bravo, Charlie, and Delta.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the useful focus. Valley Tower is Class Delta. Before entry, the pilot must establish two-way radio communication with the ATC facility providing service and maintain that communication while inside. Class Delta by itself generally requires an operable two-way radio, but not the transponder and ADS-B Out package associated with Class Charlie. Nearby airspace can still create separate equipment requirements, so we will check equipment against the whole route in the next section.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Imagine the first call: “Valley Tower, Cessna One Two Three Alpha Bravo, eight miles west, three thousand five hundred, landing with the weather.” If the controller replies, “Cessna One Two Three Alpha Bravo, standby,” the AIM says two-way communication has been established because the response used the aircraft identification. For Class Delta, the airplane may enter unless the controller instructs it to remain outside.

**LEARNER:**

What if the reply is only, “Aircraft calling Valley Tower, standby”?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The AIM says communication has not been established when the controller’s response does not include the aircraft identification. The pilot remains outside and resolves the communication before crossing the boundary.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Now move the same exchange to Class Charlie. For a typical arrival or through flight, Class Charlie also requires two-way communication before entry. The callsign test works the same way: “Cessna One Two Three Alpha Bravo, standby” establishes communication. A reply that omits the aircraft identification does not. Class Charlie also generally brings an altitude-reporting transponder and ADS-B Out equipment requirement.

**LEARNER:**

Then Class Bravo is the same call, but with more equipment?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

No. This is the entry distinction worth saying plainly. Class Charlie and Class Delta use established two-way communication. Class Bravo requires an explicit ATC clearance before entry. Hearing your callsign, receiving a transponder code, or being told to standby is not the Class Bravo clearance. The pilot waits for language that actually clears the airplane into the Class Bravo airspace.

**LEARNER:**

That gives me three different entry answers: no general VFR entry call for Echo or Golf, communication established for Charlie and Delta, and an explicit clearance for Bravo.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. The PHAK’s Figure 15-9 puts those entry requirements beside the usual pilot and equipment requirements. Keep that figure open from the show notes while studying this section.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Because many listeners are student pilots, Class Bravo also has a training boundary. Section 61.95 requires specific ground and flight training and a current logbook endorsement for the particular Class Bravo area. It also prohibits student-pilot operations at the airports named in that regulation. A student planning Class Bravo work should identify the exact airspace and airport with a CFI, rather than treating a general endorsement as nationwide access.

**LEARNER:**

The class name tells me where to begin, but the chart and regulation finish the decision.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. The common shapes help recognition: Class Bravo often looks like a layered wedding cake, Class Charlie commonly has a surface core and an outer shelf, and Class Delta is generally a surface area around an airport with an operating tower. Each area is individually tailored. Read its printed floors, ceilings, hours, and notes instead of forcing the common shape onto the chart.

## [13:00] Match the equipment to the route

**ANNOUNCER:**

Match the equipment to the route.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

After the Valley Tower stop, the planned route passes near Class Charlie and under a Class Bravo shelf. The airplane is not entering either shelf, but that does not finish the equipment check. Equipment rules can follow the class, extend above it, or apply inside a separate area drawn around it.

**LEARNER:**

Let’s define the equipment before we place it on the route.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

An altitude-reporting transponder responds to ATC radar interrogation and reports the airplane’s pressure altitude when operating in the applicable mode. Pilots often call the altitude-reporting capability Mode Charlie. ADS-B Out is the aircraft’s broadcast of position and related information. The word “Out” matters: receiving traffic or weather through ADS-B In does not establish that the airplane has the required ADS-B Out installation.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

For the common domestic operations in this scenario, section 91.215 requires an altitude-reporting transponder in Classes Alpha, Bravo, and Charlie; above Class Bravo and Charlie within their lateral limits up to 10,000 feet MSL; within the 30-nautical-mile Mode Charlie veil around listed Class Bravo primary airports; and at or above 10,000 feet MSL, except in the airspace at and below 2,500 feet above the surface. The regulation contains exceptions, so an aircraft that may qualify for one must be checked against the full text before the route is accepted.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Section 91.225 places ADS-B Out requirements in the same major areas for this route: Classes Alpha, Bravo, and Charlie; above Class Bravo and Charlie within their lateral limits up to 10,000 feet MSL; inside the Mode Charlie veil; and at or above 10,000 feet MSL, excluding the airspace at and below 2,500 feet above the surface. It also includes other locations not part of this inland scenario. The exact regulation and the airplane’s equipment records answer whether a particular installation and route comply.

**LEARNER:**

So the shelf and the veil are not the same boundary.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. The Class Bravo shelves define Class Bravo airspace. The Mode Charlie veil is generally a 30-nautical-mile equipment area from the surface to 10,000 feet MSL around a listed Class Bravo primary airport. The Chart Users’ Guide shows it as a solid magenta boundary. Our training airplane can remain below the Class Bravo shelf and still be inside the veil, where the transponder and ADS-B Out requirements apply unless an exception or authorization changes the result.

**LEARNER:**

Then “I am not entering Bravo” is only an airspace-entry answer. It is not an equipment answer.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Run the equipment check against the complete horizontal route and planned altitude. Then ask what happens if weather, traffic, or terrain produces a climb or reroute. A route that is legal only while the airplane holds a narrow altitude under a shelf leaves little room for an ordinary change.

## [18:00] Turn weather minimums into a usable picture

**ANNOUNCER:**

Turn weather minimums into a usable picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The equipment check is complete, but the weather has changed along the route. Before choosing a row from the VFR minimums table, define the measurements in that row.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Flight visibility is the forward visibility judged from the cockpit in flight. Ground visibility is the visibility reported at an airport. Section 91.155 uses flight visibility for an airplane in flight. When ground visibility is reported at an airport, that reported value controls whether an airplane may take off or land under the basic VFR rule.

**LEARNER:**

So a nearby airport reporting four miles does not prove that I have four miles of flight visibility where I am.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. The report is evidence at the reporting location. The pilot still evaluates the view from the airplane and the conditions along the segment.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Cloud clearance is the required vertical or horizontal distance between the airplane and a cloud. “Clear of clouds” does not assign a number, but it still means the airplane cannot enter a cloud. The pilot must also keep enough visibility and room to see traffic, terrain, and a usable path.

**LEARNER:**

And now the second meaning of ceiling?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

For aviation weather, a ceiling is the height above ground level of the lowest layer reported as broken or overcast, or the vertical visibility into an obscuration. A scattered layer is not a ceiling. If a report says scattered at 800 and overcast at 1,500, the ceiling is 1,500 feet AGL.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Below 10,000 feet MSL, Class Bravo requires three statute miles of visibility and clear of clouds. Classes Charlie, Delta, and Echo require three statute miles, 500 feet below clouds, 1,000 feet above, and 2,000 feet horizontally. That second group is commonly remembered as “three, one-five-two”: three miles; one thousand above; five hundred below; two thousand horizontal. The order in the mnemonic is not the order in the table, so rebuild the actual directions rather than repeating four numbers without meaning.

**LEARNER:**

The airplane needs more room below a cloud than above it: 500 feet below and 1,000 feet above.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Say the direction with each number. A bare “one-five-two” is easy to reverse when the weather is already demanding attention.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Class Golf changes with height above the surface and with day or night. At 1,200 feet or less above the surface, daytime airplane operations require one statute mile and clear of clouds. At night in that layer, the common requirement is three miles and the same 500-below, 1,000-above, 2,000-horizontal distances. More than 1,200 feet above the surface but below 10,000 feet MSL, daytime Class Golf keeps the one-mile visibility but uses the cloud distances; nighttime Class Golf uses three miles and those same distances.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

At or above 10,000 feet MSL, Class Echo—and Class Golf when more than 1,200 feet above the surface—requires five statute miles, 1,000 feet below, 1,000 feet above, and one statute mile horizontally from clouds. That higher-altitude row is “five, one-one-one,” but again, attach each number to its direction and condition.

**LEARNER:**

This is why I cannot ask only, “What class am I in?” For Golf I also need day or night, height above the surface, and whether I am below or at 10,000 feet MSL.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. And there is one more surface-area rule. Except under Special VFR, an airplane may not operate beneath a ceiling under VFR within the lateral boundaries of controlled airspace designated to the surface for an airport when the ceiling is below 1,000 feet.

**LEARNER:**

That rule uses the weather ceiling we just defined, and only inside the lateral boundary of controlled airspace designated to the surface for the airport.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Notice how much clarity comes from naming the volume and the measurement. “Eight hundred overcast” is an 800-foot AGL ceiling. “Class Delta surface area” identifies where the surface-area rule applies. The pilot now has a specific legal question, not a vague sense that the weather is low.

Apply the minimums to one changing flight.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Return to Meadow Creek. It is daytime. The airport lies in Class Golf from the surface to 700 feet AGL, with Class Echo above. The automated report gives two miles visibility and scattered clouds at 900 feet. At the surface, the reported visibility exceeds the one-mile Class Golf minimum, and scattered clouds do not create a ceiling.

**LEARNER:**

But a normal climb would enter Class Echo at 700 feet AGL. Then the requirement becomes three miles with the numbered cloud clearances. Two miles does not support that climb under basic VFR.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. The fact that takeoff might fit one Class Golf row does not establish that the planned departure can continue. The pilot evaluates the route through every layer the airplane needs to use.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Even when the numbers meet the legal minimum, ask whether the conditions leave a workable safety margin. One mile of visibility in daytime Class Golf can be legal in the stated layer, but it gives little time to identify terrain, towers, traffic, or worsening weather. A personal minimum should reflect the pilot’s experience, the terrain, available alternatives, and the work required during that segment.

**LEARNER:**

So the regulation answers whether a floor has been crossed. It does not tell me that flying at that floor is a good plan.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. A minimum is a boundary, not a performance prediction. In flight, visibility can vary ahead of the airplane, and a cloud-distance requirement does not guarantee that the remaining corridor leads somewhere useful. If the route is narrowing between terrain, airspace, and clouds, make the decision while an easy turn, landing, or diversion is still available.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Later, approaching Valley Tower’s Class Delta surface area, the weather report changes to two miles visibility with an 800-foot overcast ceiling. Basic VFR needs three miles in Class Delta. The ceiling is also below 1,000 feet, so the surface-area ceiling rule prevents ordinary VFR operation beneath it within that airspace.

**LEARNER:**

That brings up Special VFR, but it should not erase the rest of the weather picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Let’s examine it as a separate decision.

## [27:00] Use Special VFR as a separate decision

**ANNOUNCER:**

Use Special VFR as a separate decision.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Special VFR, often shortened to S-V-F-R, is an ATC clearance for operation within a Class Bravo, Charlie, Delta, or Echo surface area when the weather is below basic VFR minimums and the regulatory conditions can be met. For an airplane, the operation requires at least one statute mile visibility and must remain clear of clouds. Between sunset and sunrise, the pilot must be instrument rated and the airplane equipped for instrument flight.

**LEARNER:**

In our daytime scenario, two miles and 800 overcast might fit the visibility number if the airplane can remain clear of clouds. Does that make the arrival a good Special VFR plan?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

It makes Special VFR a regulatory option that may be requested and may be issued when traffic and the local rules permit. It does not answer the larger safety question. The pilot still needs visual contact with terrain and obstacles, room to remain clear of clouds, manageable workload, and a safe path to the airport. Some Class Bravo and Charlie airports prohibit fixed-wing Special VFR, and those locations are identified in the regulations and on charts.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The AIM also explains that a Special VFR clearance is effective only within the surface area. After the airplane leaves that boundary, the clearance does not establish that the next airspace or route segment meets basic VFR minimums. If a departure depends on one mile and clear of clouds inside the surface area but immediately encounters Class Echo requiring three miles outside it, the route still fails.

**LEARNER:**

Then Special VFR should not become a way to launch and discover whether the weather beyond the boundary is usable.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. In this scenario, the destination has an alternate with better weather behind the airplane. The early, lower-workload choice is to use that alternate instead of compressing the route between a low ceiling, reduced visibility, unfamiliar terrain, and the edge of a clearance. During flight instruction, work with your CFI on how Special VFR appears in local operations and on the visual references and escape choices needed before such a clearance would be considered.

## [31:00] Treat every special-use boundary according to its meaning

**ANNOUNCER:**

Treat every special-use boundary according to its meaning.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The alternate route avoids the low weather but approaches an area outlined for military activity. “Special use airspace,” or S-U-A, is not one rule. The charted type tells the pilot what kind of activity or restriction the boundary represents.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Prohibited areas contain airspace where flight is prohibited. Restricted areas identify unusual, often invisible hazards such as firing or missiles; entry when the restriction is active requires authorization from the controlling or using agency. Warning areas extend offshore and warn of activity that may be hazardous to nonparticipating aircraft.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

A military operations area, or M-O-A, separates certain military training activities from IFR traffic. VFR flight is not generally prohibited, but the AIM advises pilots to obtain current activity information and exercise extreme caution while an M-O-A is active. An alert area identifies high-volume pilot training or unusual aerial activity and calls for added collision-avoidance attention. A controlled firing area does not appear on the chart because its hazardous activity must stop when a nonparticipating aircraft approaches.

**LEARNER:**

For the M-O-A near our route, the useful question is not simply whether I may cross the line. I need its vertical limits, scheduled times, current status, the type of activity, and a plan that does not depend on seeing fast traffic at the last moment.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the safety decision. The AIM provides contact information for obtaining current status. If the area is active, an uncomplicated route around it may be a better choice for a training flight even when VFR transit is not prohibited.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The chart also shows a military training route, or M-T-R. The PHAK explains that these routes commonly support military operations below 10,000 feet MSL at speeds above 250 knots. They are labeled I-R or V-R followed by numbers, and the number of digits helps indicate whether a segment extends above 1,500 feet AGL. The route symbol is a prompt to check current information and increase traffic awareness, not a wall drawn around an empty corridor.

**LEARNER:**

And the temporary flight restriction did not appear on the sectional at all.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. A temporary flight restriction, or T-F-R, is established and described through a current NOTAM. The NOTAM gives the location, effective time, dimensions, affected altitudes, reason, and coordination information. A sectional may show some recurring TFR sites, but the chart alone cannot establish whether a temporary restriction affects today’s flight.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That makes the TFR check different from recognizing a permanent airspace boundary. The pilot checks current NOTAMs during planning, plots the active volume in three dimensions, reads who may operate and under what conditions, and changes the route before takeoff when the planned segment is not permitted.

**LEARNER:**

Where do Special Air Traffic Rules and Special Flight Rules Areas fit?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

A Special Air Traffic Rules area, or S-A-T-R, and a Special Flight Rules Area, or S-F-R-A, apply special local operating rules. The underlying class of airspace does not tell the whole story. A route that touches one requires the current area-specific rules, charting, training, filing, communication, or equipment information that applies there. This episode establishes the recognition step; it does not compress different local programs into one generic procedure.

Retrieval review.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The ACS asks you to identify airspace and apply its VFR weather, communication, equipment, and special-use requirements. What four questions did we use to organize that work?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

At Meadow Creek, Class Golf reaches the surface and Class Echo begins at 700 feet AGL. What happens during the climb?

**LEARNER:**

The airplane can cross from Class Golf into Class Echo without changing its horizontal position. I convert the 700-foot AGL floor into an MSL altitude using local elevation, then apply the Class Echo requirements after crossing it.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Which classes are controlled, and what common misunderstanding should you avoid?

**LEARNER:**

Classes Alpha through Echo are controlled, while Golf is uncontrolled. Controlled airspace means ATC services are provided according to the classification; it does not mean every VFR aircraft has an individual clearance or separation service.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

What is the entry distinction among Class Delta, Class Charlie, and Class Bravo?

**LEARNER:**

Delta and Charlie require two-way radio communication before entry. A controller response using my aircraft identification establishes it, including “callsign, standby.” Bravo requires an explicit ATC clearance before entry; a callsign or transponder code alone is not that clearance.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Why can an airplane below a Class Bravo shelf still need an altitude-reporting transponder and ADS-B Out?

**LEARNER:**

Because the Mode Charlie veil is a separate equipment area, generally within 30 nautical miles of a listed Class Bravo primary airport from the surface to 10,000 feet MSL. The shelf answers the Class Bravo entry question; the veil answers a separate equipment question.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Below 10,000 feet MSL, what are the common basic VFR weather rows for Bravo and for Charlie, Delta, and Echo?

**LEARNER:**

Class Bravo is three statute miles and clear of clouds. Classes Charlie, Delta, and Echo are three statute miles, 500 feet below, 1,000 feet above, and 2,000 feet horizontal from clouds.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

What additional variables must you identify before using the Class Golf row?

**LEARNER:**

Day or night, height above the surface, and whether the airplane is below or at 10,000 feet MSL. In flight I also evaluate flight visibility from the cockpit; when reported ground visibility exists for takeoff or landing, that report controls the ground-visibility part of the rule.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Define a weather ceiling, then apply the controlled-surface-area rule.

**LEARNER:**

A ceiling is the lowest broken or overcast layer, or vertical visibility into an obscuration, reported in height above ground level. Except under Special VFR, basic VFR flight may not operate beneath a ceiling within the lateral boundaries of controlled airspace designated to the surface for an airport when that ceiling is below 1,000 feet.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

What does a Special VFR clearance establish, and what does it leave unanswered?

**LEARNER:**

For an airplane, it can authorize operation within a controlled surface area with at least one statute mile visibility while remaining clear of clouds, subject to the other regulatory and local conditions. At night the pilot must be instrument rated and the airplane IFR equipped. The clearance ends at the surface-area boundary, so the weather, terrain, visibility, and VFR minimums beyond that boundary remain separate decisions.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Finally, how do you avoid treating all unusual chart boundaries as the same restriction?

**LEARNER:**

Identify the type, then use its rule and current status. Prohibited means flight is prohibited. Active restricted airspace requires authorization. A warning area identifies offshore hazard. An M-O-A calls for current activity information and extreme caution. An alert area calls for added traffic vigilance. A T-F-R is governed by its current NOTAM. An S-A-T-R or S-F-R-A requires the specific local rules.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That rebuilds the whole lesson. Place the airplane in a three-dimensional volume. Establish the required communication or clearance before entry. Match the pilot and equipment to the entire route, not only the airspace you intend to enter. Apply the correct visibility, cloud, and ceiling rule to every layer. Then preserve enough margin to respond when the route, weather, or traffic changes.
