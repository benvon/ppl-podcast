# Aeromedical Factors and Human Performance — research packet

## Scope and scenario

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners studying ACS PA.I.H Human Factors.
- Target duration: 30–45 minutes, subject to editorial and audio QA.
- Core question: Can the pilot recognize a physical or cognitive limit soon enough to choose a lower-demand option?
- Scenario: A planned daytime VFR cross-country with a friend and fixed arrival expectation. The pilot slept poorly, has mild congestion, and considers a cold medicine. The pilot postpones the first departure because illness, medication, and descent pressure each need assessment. On a later rested day, the pilot rechecks fitness and flies the route. In-flight heater use, a headache, and reduced alertness lead to prompt CO precautions and an early landing. Schedule pressure and task priority run through the flight; visual orientation is developed as a post-landing study bridge.
- Out of scope: individualized medical clearance, medication-specific wait times, aircraft-specific emergency procedures, exhaustive night illusions, and specialized scuba schedules. These are linked for study as appropriate.

## Source-contract decisions

- Keep illness and medication separate. FAA medication guidance says either may compromise safety and refers specific questions to an AME; there is no blanket medication clearance or generic wait interval in the script.
- Keep CO and altitude hypoxia separate mechanisms: CO reduces blood oxygen transport; reduced atmospheric pressure lowers oxygen availability. Their effects can overlap, so the scenario does not diagnose from one symptom.
- When heater use coincides with headache, drowsiness, dizziness, or exhaust odor, AIM 8-1-4 supports heater off and vents open; an early landing is the scenario's risk decision.
- AIM 8-1-2 says upper-respiratory infection or allergy can obstruct ear/sinus equalization, especially on descent. The scenario postpones rather than depending on a decongestant.
- This daytime scenario does not require a detailed night-flight lesson. Night adaptation and optical illusions are short bridges with source links.
- Retain ACS breadth in the research map, but develop at least three K1 cause/symptom/action chains so the lesson is listenable.

## Likely trouble spots

- Feeling better after a pill does not answer whether the illness or medication makes flying unsafe. Sources: FAA OTC Medications Reference Guide p. 1; AIM 8-1-1.
- Hypoxia, hyperventilation, CO, and fatigue can share vague symptoms. Sources: AIM 8-1-1 through 8-1-4.
- A legal minimum does not prove personal fitness. Sources: 14 CFR 91.17(a); AIM 8-1-1.
- Inner-ear sensations and visual scenes can mislead when outside references weaken. Source: AIM 8-1-5.
