# Cross-Country Planning, Fuel, and Diversions — research packet

## Scope

- **Audience:** U.S. private-pilot airplane learners who can read a sectional but need to connect the manual route-planning navigation log with aircraft performance, fuel, alternatives, and in-flight replanning.
- **Target duration:** 30–45 minutes; approximately 5,500–6,100 spoken words.
- **Core question:** How does a pilot build a current cross-country plan with margin and workable options, compare it with actual progress, fuel, and weather, and act early when the better option changes?
- **Out of scope:** Detailed weather-product interpretation, full sectional-symbol instruction, receiver-specific GPS programming, instrument-flight alternate rules, emergency fuel procedures, real-airport data, and aircraft-specific operating technique. Episodes 13, 16, 17, and 18 provide the detailed weather, airspace, visual-navigation, and navigation-system foundations used here.

## Teaching spine

The lesson returns to the fictional daytime VFR Meadow Ridge-to-Harbor Field flight introduced in Episode 18. In the days and hours leading up to this specific departure, current route and altitude work remains usable, Harbor is forecast above the pilot’s personal arrival minimum, and current Pine Valley weather, runway, and service information supports preparing it as a practical option. The route is one hundred forty nautical miles. The pilot manually completes a route-planning navigation log from the current chart and route, planned checkpoints and altitude, forecast wind, proposed departure time, and the fictional airplane’s approved performance data. The log carries the measured course and distance through the wind-correction and heading chain, planned groundspeed, leg and cumulative time, ETA, climb transition, and planned fuel used and remaining. The planned airplane carries thirty gallons of usable fuel, and all scenario numbers are teaching data rather than values for a real aircraft.

After departure, the airplane reaches River Bend checkpoint later than planned. Observed groundspeed is lower and fuel use is higher than the plan predicted. Updated Harbor weather has also moved below the pilot’s preselected continuation trigger while Pine Valley remains suitable. The pilot keeps the airplane and outside scan primary, turns toward the already studied alternative, marks time, makes a reasonable heading, groundspeed, arrival-time, and fuel estimate, then refines the plan with current information and available resources. The scenario ends with a landing at Pine Valley while preserving the pilot’s one-hour personal fuel floor.

The recurring decision chain is **plan, compare, act**:

1. **Prepare the inputs:** current chart, route and checkpoints, altitude, weather, airport, NOTAM, departure time, loading, performance, and fuel information.
2. **Build the prediction:** complete the manual navigation log through course, wind correction, true and magnetic headings, compass heading, groundspeed, leg and cumulative time, ETA, climb transition, trip fuel, the legal predeparture fuel check, and a personal landing-fuel floor.
3. **Prepare the options:** suitable airports, route, weather, runway, fuel, and a simple decision trigger before workload rises.
4. **Compare actual with planned:** checkpoint time, observed groundspeed, fuel used, fuel indications, weather updates, and projected landing fuel.
5. **Act on the trend:** select and turn toward the suitable diversion, then refine heading, time, fuel, weather, and communication without allowing head-down work to displace aircraft control or collision avoidance.

## Scenario numbers and arithmetic

All values below are invented teaching data. They are not a performance example for any real airplane.

- Planned usable fuel aboard: 30.0 gallons.
- Proposed departure time: 1400 local for the fictional worked log.
- Planned cruise altitude: 3,500 feet MSL.
- Main northbound cruise-row inputs: 360° true course, wind from 300° at 10 knots, and 105 knots true airspeed.
- Main northbound cruise-row outputs: about 5° left wind correction, 355° true heading, 100 knots groundspeed, 357° magnetic course and 352° magnetic heading after 3° east variation, and 353° compass heading after the fictional 1° west deviation entry.
- Planned climb: 12 minutes, 2.0 gallons, and 18 nautical miles along route.
- Planned River Bend checkpoint: 66 nautical miles cumulative; about 41 minutes elapsed and a 1441 ETA; about 6.1 gallons used and 23.9 gallons remaining.
- Planned cruise after climb: 122 nautical miles at 100 knots groundspeed, or 1 hour 13 minutes, at 8.5 gallons per hour, or 10.4 gallons.
- Planned descent and arrival allowance: 10 minutes and 1.5 gallons.
- Planned destination use: 13.9 gallons; planned destination fuel remaining: 16.1 gallons.
- Day-VFR regulatory reserve at the scenario’s 8.5-gallon-per-hour normal cruise rate: 4.25 gallons for 30 minutes after reaching the first intended landing.
- Scenario predeparture fuel-load calculation: 13.9 gallons trip fuel plus 4.25 gallons legal reserve equals 18.15 gallons required aboard before beginning the flight under the stated assumptions.
- Scenario personal landing-fuel floor: 8.5 gallons, equal to one hour at the planned normal-cruise rate.
- At River Bend: 45 minutes elapsed; 7.5 gallons used; 22.5 gallons computed remaining; observed groundspeed from the last known segment is 88 knots rather than 100.
- Revised Harbor projection: 74 nautical miles remain; about 50 minutes at 88 knots; using the observed 10-gallon-per-hour trend plus a 1.0-gallon arrival allowance projects about 13.2 gallons remaining at Harbor.
- Pine Valley diversion: about 40 nautical miles; about 27 minutes at an estimated 90-knot groundspeed; about 4.5 gallons at the observed 10-gallon-per-hour rate plus 1.0 gallon for arrival; projected landing fuel about 17.0 gallons.

The numerical comparison is intentionally generous enough that the teaching decision is not “stretch the tank.” The diversion is driven by early recognition of a combined weather, progress, and fuel trend while a clearly suitable option remains available.

## Likely trouble spots

- **Misconception:** The direct route is automatically the most efficient plan.
  - **Correction:** A usable route includes airspace, terrain, checkpoints, wind, airport suitability, alternatives, and aircraft performance. A slightly longer route can be operationally better if it improves those relationships.
  - **Sources:** ACS PA.I.D; PHAK Chapter 16, pp. 16-17–16-18.
- **Misconception:** An EFB result removes the need to understand or verify the navigation-log inputs.
  - **Correction:** PHAK p. 16-18 permits formulas, manual computers, and electronic computers. Manual practice is presented as a learning and cross-check method, not an FAA manual-only rule; either method still depends on the current route, wind, altitude, timing, and aircraft data entered.
  - **Sources:** PHAK Chapter 16, pp. 16-18 and 16-20.
- **Misconception:** Multiplying cruise fuel flow by total trip time completes the fuel plan.
  - **Correction:** Use applicable climb, cruise, descent or arrival, and reserve information from the airplane’s approved data and the planned conditions. Keep the inputs and units visible.
  - **Sources:** PHAK Chapter 11, pp. 11-1, 11-21, and 11-24; PHAK Chapter 16, p. 16-20.
- **Misconception:** The regulatory reserve is the pilot’s only fuel decision.
  - **Correction:** Section 91.151 checks the required predeparture fuel load for the stated VFR conditions. The pilot can choose a separate personal landing-fuel floor and project landing fuel as actual groundspeed, fuel use, weather, and alternatives change.
  - **Sources:** 14 CFR 91.151(a); ACS PA.I.D.R6/S4; PHAK Chapter 16, p. 16-21.
- **Misconception:** A fuel totalizer or planning app proves how much usable fuel is aboard.
  - **Correction:** Begin with a confirmed onboard quantity using the aircraft-specific preflight method, compare the cockpit indications, and then use computed burn and instrument information as cross-checks.
  - **Source:** PHAK Chapter 7, p. 7-26.
- **Misconception:** An alternate is only an airport name written on the flight plan.
  - **Correction:** A practical alternative has current weather, runway, route, performance, fuel, services, and communications information that lets the pilot act without beginning a new research project in the air.
  - **Sources:** 14 CFR 91.103; PHAK Chapter 16, p. 16-17; ACS PA.VI.C.
- **Misconception:** Finish the calculation before turning toward the diversion.
  - **Correction:** After selecting a suitable destination, the ACS expects a prompt diversion. Control the airplane, turn toward the safe general direction, mark time, make reasonable estimates, then refine them while preserving the outside scan.
  - **Sources:** ACS PA.VI.C.S1-S6; PHAK Chapter 2, p. 2-24.
- **Misconception:** ATC or the destination tower automatically activates or closes a VFR flight plan.
  - **Correction:** The pilot is responsible for activation and closure; ATC does not know the plan’s active status merely because radar service is being provided.
  - **Sources:** AIM 5-1-5 and 5-1-14; 14 CFR 91.153(b).

## Editorial risks to avoid

- Do not repeat all of Episode 17’s chart-symbol or wind-triangle instruction or Episode 18’s receiver operation. Name that material as the foundation, then focus on completing the Core 19 navigation-log workflow and using its results for performance, fuel, alternatives, monitoring, and diversion.
- Do not present the fictional thirty-gallon load, fuel flow, speeds, or times as Skyhawk data or as safe numbers for another airplane.
- Do not describe the thirty-minute day-VFR reserve as a target to consume or as an in-flight legal minimum that section 91.151 continuously recalculates. State its exact departure scope, then teach the operational projection separately.
- Do not imply that an EFB calculation verifies source currency, airplane loading, fuel actually aboard, or airport suitability. Each input must be established before the output is useful.
- Do not let a diversion become a cockpit arithmetic demonstration. The aircraft-control, collision-avoidance, and workload sequence comes before refinement.
- Do not teach “nearest” as synonymous with “suitable.” The destination and route need current weather, runway, performance, terrain, airspace, fuel, and operational consideration.
- Do not repeat the AI-assisted notice or add production status prose to show notes or the master script.
