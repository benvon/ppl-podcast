# VOR, GPS, Radar Services, and Navigation Systems — research packet

## Scope

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners who have completed Episode 16, *Airspace, Equipment, and VFR Weather Minimums*, and Episode 17, *Charts, Pilotage, and Dead Reckoning*.
- Target duration: 30–45 minutes, approximately 4,200–6,300 spoken words.
- Core question: How does a VFR pilot prepare VOR, DME, GPS, ADS-B traffic information, and VFR radar services, then use them in sequence to build a position and traffic picture?
- Scenario: A fictional daytime VFR airplane departs Meadow Ridge for Harbor Field. Before departure, the pilot prepares current chart checkpoints, a panel VOR/CDI and DME reference, a panel GPS route, ADS-B traffic equipment, and an approach-control contact plan. After Harbor VOR, a GPS and visual checkpoint need comparison; closer to Harbor Field, the pilot uses traffic information and requested VFR radar service. The route, airports, times, equipment, calls, and conditions are teaching data, not an operational procedure or a real flight.
- Out of scope: Instrument procedures, DME/DME RNAV operating guidance, IFR GPS approvals and database rules, exact interception or tracking technique, GPS programming sequence, loss-of-navigation or emergency procedures, ADS-B equipment mandates and route-trigger rules, electronic flight bags, particular equipment supplements, and diversion planning. Episode 16 owns ADS-B installation, terminology, and equipment-requirement planning. A Sentry/ForeFlight reference is a product-specific documentation link rather than general equipment instruction.

## Source-led teaching chain

1. Start with the ACS decision and a short reminder of Episode 17 planning: current route picture, checkpoints, weather, and alternatives.
2. Prepare the physical inputs before departure: GPS route and current-data question; then VOR frequency, sectional identifier, Morse pattern, station identification, usable-signal check, and DME availability.
3. Build the VOR/DME mental picture: radials outward, selected inbound course, CDI or HSI indication, direct-line DME range, line-of-sight coverage, and station passage.
4. Add current FAA context: VOR MON as the conventional fallback for aircraft unable to continue RNAV, including aircraft without DME/DME equipment; DME/DME RNAV as a separate capability for suitably equipped aircraft; and NextGen sustainment of high-power DME for RNAV en-route procedures. Keep this as architecture context for a general-aviation VFR learner, not procedural fallback guidance.
5. Use GPS as the next position source, with integrity, data currency, visual checkpoint, VOR/DME, and dead-reckoning comparisons.
6. Make the Episode 16 callback brief. Core 18 distinguishes direct ADS-B traffic from ground-delivered TIS-B, explains that the source distinction does not delay the immediate visual search, and reserves source, coverage, and completeness questions for equipment preparation and review. Product-specific receiver behavior stays in manufacturer documentation.
7. Establish the flight-following relationship before teaching VFR radar traffic information, vectoring, workload, coverage, and the pilot's communication role. When ATC can provide radar advisories, distinguish the computer-assigned beacon-code step, pilot operation on the ATC-specified Mode 3/A code with altitude reporting enabled, and radar-identification steps without teaching phraseology or buttonology.
8. End with the whole Meadow Ridge–Harbor sequence in flight order.

## Likely trouble spots

- Misconception: A VOR radial is a line pointing toward the station.
  - Correction: A radial extends outward from the VOR; an inbound course is the reciprocal of the relevant radial.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 16, p. 16-22.
- Misconception: A centered CDI alone establishes the complete navigation picture.
  - Correction: Identify the station, confirm usable signal status, name the selected course and TO/FROM sense, and compare the indication with the prepared route.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 16, pp. 16-23–16-24.
- Misconception: GPS accuracy on the moving map makes the chart, pilotage, and dead reckoning obsolete.
  - Correction: The PHAK directs VFR GPS integration with other electronic navigation, pilotage, and dead reckoning.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 16, p. 16-32.
- Misconception: A standalone DME distance display means the airplane is equipped for DME/DME RNAV.
  - Correction: DME/DME RNAV requires suitable equipment that uses multiple high-power DME facilities; the VOR MON remains the broad conventional fallback for aircraft unable to continue RNAV during a GNSS disruption.
  - Source: AIM 1-1-3.f.1; FAA Ground-Based Navigation DME program page, High-Power DME through NEXTGEN DME program.
- Misconception: Flight following means ATC will see every conflict and provide separation.
  - Correction: Radar service depends on coverage and workload; basic service does not provide standard radar separation, and VFR pilots retain see-and-avoid responsibility.
  - Source: AIM 4-1-15 and 4-1-18.
- Misconception: A flight-following request is complete once the pilot and controller exchange route information.
  - Correction: When radar advisories are provided, FAA controller procedures call for a computer-assigned beacon code; the pilot uses the ATC-specified Mode 3/A code with altitude reporting enabled, a requested code change can establish radar identification, and controllers establish and maintain identification before radar service.
  - Source: AIM 4-1-20, Automatic Altitude Reporting, paragraph a.2; FAA Order JO 7110.65BB, paragraphs 5-2-7.a, 5-3-1, and 5-3-3.a.2.
- Misconception: Every ADS-B In display receives the same traffic sources.
  - Correction: Direct air-to-air ADS-B traffic, TIS-B, and ADS-R have different receiver, client, link, and coverage conditions; review the installed or portable equipment documentation.
  - Source: AIM 4-5-8.

## Editorial risks to avoid

- Treating a single panel layout, control, annunciation pattern, or portable receiver capability as universal. Describe the functional question and defer aircraft-specific operation to the AFM/POH, equipment supplement, manufacturer documentation, and CFI.
- Turning VOR tracking or GPS programming into a procedure memorized from a podcast.
- Calling requested radar traffic service “flight following” as though it were a defined, guaranteed service. Use the familiar term while naming the requested VFR radar advisory service and its conditions.
- Collapsing traffic information, basic radar service, vectoring, TRSA service, Class B, and Class C service into one promise.
- Repeating Episode 16's installation, terminology, or regulatory-route discussion instead of connecting direct ADS-B traffic, TIS-B, surveillance coverage, visual acquisition, and the outside scan to one safety decision.
