## In this episode

- Why a stall is an angle-of-attack event, rather than an airspeed-indicator event.
- Why the airspeed associated with a stall changes with conditions.
- How load factor, coordination, configuration, weight, center of gravity, power, and yaw relate to stall risk.
- Common stall cues, including the limits of stall-warning devices.
- The aerodynamic priority in recovery and why the approved POH/AFM controls the procedure.
- Why a stalled, yawed airplane can enter a spin and how to avoid the conditions that lead there.

## What to retain

1. A wing stalls when it exceeds critical angle of attack. The airspeed at which that happens is condition-dependent.
2. A published one-G stall speed is useful reference data, not a universal safety boundary.
3. Increased load factor can make a stall occur at a higher airspeed. A bank angle alone is not the whole story.
4. Power can help manage a recovery but does not, by itself, unstall the wing. Reducing angle of attack is the immediate aerodynamic priority; use the approved aircraft procedure.
5. A spin involves a stalled, yawed condition. Coordination, early recognition, and declining an unstable approach or climb plan are central prevention habits.

## Scenario questions

1. You are above the published stall speed but in a climbing, turning, gusty flight condition. Why does the published number alone not settle the stall-risk question?
2. You are overshooting final from base. What control inputs create a cross-control risk, and what decision avoids treating runway alignment as more important than aircraft control?
3. Why is adding power not a substitute for reducing angle of attack in a stall?
4. What makes a spin different from a spiral dive?
