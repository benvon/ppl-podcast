**INSTRUCTOR:**

Suppose you overshoot the planned turn toward final. Your airspeed is above the stall speed you remember from the handbook. Does that establish that a tighter, harder-pulled turn is safe?

**LEARNER:**

I know the remembered speed needs context, but I want to explain the connection correctly. What is changing when I pull harder in the turn?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Picture the wing meeting the oncoming air. The chord is a line from the wing's leading edge to its trailing edge. The angle between that line and the relative wind is the angle of attack. When that angle exceeds the wing's critical angle, airflow separates, lift decreases, and the wing stalls. The Airplane Flying Handbook explains that exceeding critical angle of attack causes the stall; a single airspeed value does not define it in every condition.
[Source: sources.yaml#afh-angle-of-attack]

In a coordinated turn that maintains altitude, increasing bank requires more lift. The handbook explains that the pilot increases angle of attack to produce that lift, increasing load factor and the speed at which the wing can stall. Its example of a sixty-degree level turn illustrates that relationship. We do not need that bank angle on Elena's arrival; it shows why the level-flight stall speed cannot answer the harder-pulled-turn question.
[Source: sources.yaml#afh-angle-of-attack]

**LEARNER:**

Then I should recognize that pulling harder can use up the angle-of-attack margin even while the airspeed is above the number I had in mind. I would avoid trying to rescue this approach with an abrupt turn. For our scenario, I choose the go-around and establish another controlled path.
[Source: sources.yaml#afh-angle-of-attack]
[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is how an aerodynamic answer becomes an operational choice. On the actual test, the ACS power-off-stall task connects angle of attack, airspeed, load factor, configuration, weight, and yaw with recognition, prevention, and the airplane's recovery procedure. Your practice with your instructor needs to connect the explanation to those flight skills.
[Source: sources.yaml#acs-stalls]

**LEARNER:**

If I choose the go-around, what should I be able to explain besides the word itself?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

When you recognized the need, how you apply the airplane's power and climb guidance, how you manage configuration, and how you avoid conflicting traffic while maintaining control. The ACS go-around task includes timely recognition, power application, configuration, airspeed, directional control, and collision avoidance. A go-around is another phase to manage deliberately.
[Source: sources.yaml#acs-go-around]

**LEARNER:**
