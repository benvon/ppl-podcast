# Private-Pilot Privileges, Limitations, and Currency — research packet

## Scope

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners preparing to answer an ACS Pilot Qualifications scenario.
- Target duration: 21–24 minutes, based on the current narration length and recent episode pacing.
- Core question: May Elena act as pilot in command of this airplane, with this passenger, on this day-and-evening trip, under the proposed expense arrangement; and is she proficient enough to accept it?
- Out of scope: commercial operations, every special exception in 14 CFR 61.113, medical diagnosis, an exhaustive BasicMed tutorial, IFR currency, rental-company checkout policies, and aircraft airworthiness or Part 91 operating rules already assigned to other episodes.

## Primary scenario trace

1. Two days before departure Elena agrees to take her friend Jonah to a daytime event, with a possible return after dark. She retrieves her certificate, photo ID, medical basis, flight-review record, logbook, aircraft details, and proposed plan before promising a departure.
2. The airplane offered first has an engine rated above 200 horsepower. Elena checks the rating on her certificate and the separate high-performance training and endorsement requirement. She chooses the familiar training airplane instead and later schedules instruction in the other airplane.
3. She checks the preceding 90 days of takeoffs and landings for passenger carriage. A recent solo flight can establish the required recency if it meets the regulation's aircraft and control conditions. The evening return raises a separate night-passenger recency question tied to one hour after sunset through one hour before sunrise. Lacking that recency, she chooses a personal cutoff of landing back home before sunset.
4. Jonah offers to cover the airplane rental. Elena tests the proposed arrangement against the private-pilot compensation limit and the pro-rata rule's narrow list of expenses. She pays at least half of the qualifying operating expenses for a two-person flight.
5. Elena checks whether her third-class medical is valid for private-pilot privileges. The BasicMed branch explains how a pilot using it checks the specific qualification and flight limits rather than treating a driver's license alone as enough.
6. With all legal gates addressed, Elena still asks whether her recent experience in that airplane and avionics makes the flight sensible. She can arrange a refresher with an instructor, use the familiar airplane, and keep the agreed landing before sunset.

## Source contract and useful locators

- The ACS defines what the applicant must connect; it does not itself create flight permission. The operative rules are the cited Part 61 sections.
- 14 CFR 61.3(a)(1)–(2) requires the certificate or accepted temporary authority and photo identification to be in physical possession or readily accessible in the aircraft for the U.S. civil-aircraft case taught here.
- 14 CFR 61.31(c)–(d) sets rating requirements; paragraph (f)(1) separately defines a high-performance airplane as having an engine of more than 200 horsepower and specifies training and the one-time logbook endorsement, subject to paragraph (f)(2) exceptions. The scenario states Elena has no such prior qualification.
- 14 CFR 61.56(c) uses the beginning of the 24th calendar month before the month of the flight, with stated alternatives in (d), (e), and (g). Do not call it a 24-month rolling day count.
- 14 CFR 61.57(a)(1) has three takeoffs and three landings within the preceding 90 days for PIC carriage of persons, with sole-manipulator and category/class/type conditions; the tailwheel-airplane case requires full-stop landings. Elena's chosen airplane is non-tailwheel, so the core claim is scoped to her case. Paragraph (b)(1) separately requires three takeoffs and landings to a full stop within the preceding 90 days in the specified one-hour-after-sunset to one-hour-before-sunrise window for PIC carriage of persons in that period. Do not equate this passenger-currency window with every definition of night.
- 14 CFR 61.113(a) limits private-pilot compensation or hire; (b) addresses flight merely incidental to business or employment and is not needed for Elena's social trip; (c) allows expense sharing only when the private pilot pays at least a pro-rata share of fuel, oil, airport expenditures, or rental fees. Do not teach the expense rule as permission for a flight arranged for compensation or hire.
- 14 CFR 61.23(a)(3) requires at least a third-class medical for typical private privileges unless operating under 61.113(i). FAA AME Guide, General Information - Validity of Medical Certificates, item 3, gives the third-class remainder-of-month rule and the 24- or 60-calendar-month duration based on age at examination.
- BasicMed requires more than a driver's license: check 61.23(c)(3) prerequisites and timing, then 61.113(i) aircraft, passenger, altitude, location, and speed limits. Current FAA BasicMed summary reflects the 2024 expansion to seven occupants and 12,500 pounds. The script does not purport to enumerate all disqualifying conditions.
- 14 CFR 61.51(a)(2) requires documentation of aeronautical experience used for recent flight experience. This supports using a logbook to verify rather than reconstructing flights from memory.

## Likely trouble spots

- Misconception: A private pilot certificate alone settles the whole flight. Correction: the operation, aircraft, current medical basis, flight review, and passenger carriage requirements are separate checks.
- Misconception: A completed flight review automatically establishes three recent landings. Correction: each rule has its own evidence; a review may also include qualifying landings, but check what was actually accomplished and logged.
- Misconception: Any takeoffs and landings after sunset meet night-passenger currency. Correction: use the one-hour-after-sunset through one-hour-before-sunrise window and full-stop landings in the required aircraft category/class/type.
- Misconception: A passenger may cover all flight operating expenses because this is a social flight. Correction: Elena must pay at least her pro-rata share of the total listed operating expenses; the larger compensation question remains.
- Misconception: BasicMed means an ordinary driver's license alone qualifies a pilot. Correction: confirm the listed medical eligibility conditions, course, examination, record, and operation limits in the current rules.
- Misconception: Regulatory currency proves skill in unfamiliar avionics. Correction: compare the actual task load and recent practice with the intended aircraft and flight; use instruction or a simpler plan where needed.

## Editorial risks to avoid

- Do not turn the opening into a catalogue of sections or dates.
- Keep the social passenger arrangement distinct from business, charity, search-and-rescue, or other special 61.113 cases; link the full regulation in show notes.
- Do not imply that a rental checkout is a federal endorsement or that a high-performance endorsement is a rating.
- Do not use the sunset-to-sunrise definition of night to restate 61.57(b)'s narrower passenger-currency period.
- Do not claim that BasicMed relaxes the flight-review or passenger recency requirements.
- Keep the scenario's choices in time order and separate legal eligibility from practical proficiency.
