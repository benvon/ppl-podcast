## [18:30] Resolve conflicts before they become close

**ANNOUNCER:**

Resolve conflicts before they become close.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right of way works only after you identify the geometry, but it never replaces see and avoid. The first questions are, “Where is the other aircraft, where is it going, how will our paths meet, and do I actually have it in sight?” Put yourself back on Pine Valley’s east-side downwind, flying north at pattern altitude. A small airplane east of you is maneuvering west toward the same downwind line. It appears to your right and its track would cross ahead of you. That is a drawable convergence picture, not merely an airplane somewhere in the sky.

**LEARNER:**

In that exact picture, which aircraft gives way?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

For aircraft of the same category converging at approximately the same altitude, except in a head-on situation, the aircraft that has the other on its right must give way. In our picture, the training airplane has the other aircraft on its right, so the training pilot gives way. The rule also says a pilot giving way may not pass over, under, or ahead of the other aircraft unless well clear.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

Giving way in this picture normally means not trying to cross in front. The training pilot changes the plan early, lets the other airplane pass ahead, and remains or becomes positioned behind it with a growing visual gap. That describes the useful geometry, not a regulation-prescribed maneuver.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**LEARNER:**

What is the early response here, before we are trying to judge “well clear” at the last second?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Before joining, the pilot uses the clear reentry plan we just discussed.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

If a convergence develops while the airplane is already in the pattern, the pilot keeps the other aircraft in sight and does not try to force a crossing ahead.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

Before joining downwind, the AFH says to adjust course or speed to fit the traffic pattern. If conflicting traffic prevents a forty-five-degree entry, its supported choice is to turn away from downwind, fly a safe distance away, scan, and return for another forty-five-degree entry attempt.

[Source: sources.yaml#afh-conflicting-entry]

If the conflict is at the base-to-final decision, the AFH says to avoid close proximity to aircraft already established on final before turning final.

[Source: sources.yaml#afh-base-final]

Before joining, AC 90-66C recommends reaching pattern altitude, taking time to observe traffic, and using the standard forty-five-degree downwind entry abeam the midpoint of the runway to be used for landing.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-standard-entry]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Now separate convergence from head-on. A head-on or nearly head-on picture means both aircraft are approaching each other, not crossing toward a common point from different directions. In that case, each pilot must alter course to the right.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**LEARNER:**

And overtaking is different again because one aircraft is coming from behind, rather than meeting from the side.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. An aircraft overtaking another has the right of way responsibility and must alter course to the right to pass well clear. Identifying the picture before reciting a rule matters because “the other aircraft is on my right” does not answer a head-on or overtaking question.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

There is also a priority hierarchy that is easy to compress incorrectly. This is the correct priority hierarchy: An aircraft in distress has right of way over all other air traffic. Separately, a balloon has priority over every other category; a glider has priority over powered aircraft; an airship has priority over other powered aircraft except an aircraft towing or refueling another aircraft; and an aircraft towing or refueling another aircraft has priority over all other powered aircraft.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**LEARNER:**

So an aircraft towing a glider is not simply “first in every situation.” Its special priority is over other powered aircraft, while the regulation separately gives a glider priority over powered aircraft.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Those categories help identify the right-of-way duty; they do not erase the need to see and avoid. Even when a pilot has priority, the pilot should not continue into a developing collision because the other aircraft ought to yield. The regulation requires vigilance when weather conditions permit, and it limits a giving-way aircraft to passing over, under, or ahead only when well clear.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**LEARNER:**

What if our Pine Valley straight-in airplane says it is two miles north, but I cannot find it before the Cherokee turns base?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The AIM states that an aircraft executing a straight-in approach does not have priority over other aircraft in the traffic pattern and must comply with 14 CFR 91.113(g).

[Source: sources.yaml#aim-straight-in-priority]

Treat the missing visual contact as information. A radio report does not make its path harmless. A pilot who cannot locate the traffic avoids turning base into its expected final-approach path, states the current position and intention on CTAF, and uses a later approach when the visual picture permits.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

If a landing approach itself becomes unsafe or a conflict cannot be resolved, AC 90-66C’s go-around guidance is concrete: continue straight ahead beyond the departure end of the runway and maintain awareness of other traffic so as not to conflict with aircraft established in the pattern. That is the point at which the pilot returns to a predictable path instead of trying to rescue a bad pattern position with an improvised turn.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-go-around]

This is the operational meaning of early resolution: use the right-of-way rules to understand responsibility, then use an outside scan and a radio call to make a concrete choice—remain clear and rejoin on a later forty-five-degree entry before downwind, allow the converging traffic to pass ahead rather than crossing in front, or discontinue an unsafe approach with the straight-ahead go-around path.

## [24:00] Land without turning priority into pressure

**ANNOUNCER:**

Land without turning priority into pressure.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Return to Pine Valley after the straight-in airplane has landed. The Cherokee is now on final for Runway One Eight, and the training airplane is on base behind it. Aircraft on final approach to land or while landing have right of way over other aircraft in flight or operating on the surface.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

When two or more aircraft are approaching an airport to land, the lower aircraft has the right of way. But that lower aircraft may not take advantage of the rule to cut in front of or overtake another aircraft on final approach to land.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

Landing priority is not permission to land so close behind another aircraft that it must rush its runway exit.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**LEARNER:**

So being lower is not permission to dive in front of the Cherokee or force a compressed final.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. A right-of-way rule describes priority in a particular geometry. It is not a promise that the spacing is comfortable. The AFH directs the pilot on base to ensure that there is no close proximity to another aircraft already established on final before turning final, and to use avoidance or a go-around rather than rush a turn that creates a collision hazard.

[Source: sources.yaml#afh-base-final]

If the Cherokee is slower than expected, its position leaves little room, or the approach becomes unsafe from a traffic conflict, the training pilot discontinues the approach and uses the predictable go-around path rather than trying to compress final. The exact maneuver technique remains aircraft-specific and belongs with the POH or AFM and flight instruction.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-go-around]

**LEARNER:**

What changes if the aircraft ahead is substantially larger?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Add wake turbulence to the spacing picture. The AIM explains that wake vortices can remain behind and below the generating aircraft, and can drift and settle. When following a larger aircraft, visual distance alone is not the full question. Consider its flight path and avoid assuming that the runway will clear the wake for you.

[Source: sources.yaml#aim-wake-turbulence]

AC 90-66C separately directs pilots to remain alert for wake turbulence and avoid flight below and behind another aircraft at low altitude.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-wake-spacing]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

At Pine Valley, the training pilot sees that following the Cherokee will leave adequate visual spacing. If the airplane ahead had been larger or the wind suggested a wake concern, the pilot would make the conservative decision while there was still time, not after turning final. The repeated habit is the same one we used at entry: make the picture simpler before it becomes close.

**LEARNER:**

A quiet frequency is not proof that the airport area is empty: another aircraft may lack a radio, so I remain especially vigilant for other aircraft.

**LEARNER:**

When weather conditions permit, I maintain vigilance to see and avoid other aircraft.

Traffic displays help, but they do not replace an outside scan.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-displays-see-avoid]

I scan deliberately and account for blind spots rather than staring through one fixed part of the windshield.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

When a potential conflict appears, what order keeps right of way useful?

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**LEARNER:**

First I identify the geometry: converging, head-on, overtaking, or approaching to land. For same-category convergence, the aircraft with the other on its right gives way; head-on aircraft both alter right; and an overtaking aircraft alters right to pass well clear.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**LEARNER:**

I account for distress separately. In the category rule, balloon precedes every other category, glider precedes powered aircraft, airship precedes other powered aircraft except towing or refueling aircraft, and towing or refueling aircraft precede other powered aircraft. But I do not wait for a priority argument to solve the conflict.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

Before joining downwind, I adjust course or speed to fit the traffic pattern. If conflicting traffic prevents a forty-five-degree entry, I can turn away from downwind, fly a safe distance away, scan, and return for another forty-five-degree entry attempt.

[Source: sources.yaml#afh-conflicting-entry]

At base or final, I discontinue an unsafe approach with the straight-ahead go-around path while continuing to scan.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-go-around]

**INSTRUCTOR:**

And what prevents landing priority from becoming pressure?

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

**LEARNER:**

Aircraft on final or landing have priority, and the lower aircraft approaching to land has priority with the rule’s limit against cutting in front of or overtaking traffic on final. I use those rules to understand the sequence, not to compress it.

[Source: sources.yaml#ecfr-right-of-way]

Landing priority is not permission to land so close behind another aircraft that it must rush its runway exit.

**LEARNER:**

If the approach cannot be resolved safely, I discontinue it.

[Claim type: teaching synthesis]

**LEARNER:**

The AC’s go-around guidance is to continue straight ahead beyond the departure end while maintaining awareness of traffic established in the pattern.

[Source: sources.yaml#ac-go-around]

If I am following a larger aircraft, I consider wake behind and below its flight path, including drift and settling.

[Source: sources.yaml#aim-wake-turbulence]

At a nontowered airport, I remain alert for wake turbulence and avoid flight below and behind another aircraft at low altitude.
