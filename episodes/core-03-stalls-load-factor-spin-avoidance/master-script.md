## [03:00] The core model: critical angle of attack

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Start with airflow. In normal flight, air meets the wing and follows a path around it. The wing produces lift as part of the overall aerodynamic force picture. As that angle increases, the wing can generally produce more lift, up to a point. Beyond a particular angle, the airflow can no longer remain attached in the way the wing needs. Lift deteriorates rapidly and drag increases. That is the stall.

**LEARNER:**

So a stall is not an engine failure. It is not necessarily a dramatic nose drop. And it is not an airspeed number by itself.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

All correct. An engine can be producing power and the wing can still stall. A stall can be mild or pronounced depending on airplane design, configuration, center of gravity, and other factors. And the airspeed at which the critical angle is reached varies with the conditions.

Here is a useful but careful way to say it: airspeed is one clue to the wing's margin, not a guarantee about how close it is to critical angle. As airspeed falls in a given configuration and flight condition, the wing usually needs a greater angle to make the lift needed. But a pilot can also reduce that margin by asking the wing to do more work, such as by increasing load factor in a turn. That is why the same airplane can stall at different indicated airspeeds.

**LEARNER:**

Then why does the POH give me a stall speed at all?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Because it is useful information under the conditions for which it is published. The problem comes when we turn a published value into a universal dividing line between “cannot stall” and “will stall.” The FAA's Airplane Flying Handbook emphasizes that a published, level-flight, one-G stall speed is valid only under its defined conditions, including unaccelerated one-G flight, coordinated flight, and the stated weight and center-of-gravity condition. Change those conditions, and the speed associated with the critical angle can change.

**LEARNER:**

So if I say, “I am five knots above the book stall speed,” I have not actually answered the safety question.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. A better question is: what is the airplane being asked to do right now, and how close might that put the wing to its critical angle? Are you turning? Pulling? Gusting? Heavy? Light? Configured differently? Coordinated? Using power? Those conditions matter.

## [06:30] Angle of attack, pitch attitude, and airspeed

**LEARNER:**

I still find it tempting to use nose attitude as a shortcut. A high nose looks like it should mean high angle of attack.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

It often accompanies it, but it is not a reliable substitute. Consider an airplane in a climbing attitude with plenty of airspeed and a relative wind that still gives the wing an angle of attack below critical. That airplane is not stalled. Now consider a different situation: an airplane is descending, the pilot abruptly pulls to change the flightpath, and the wing's angle of attack rises past critical. The visual attitude may not match the picture in your head of a traditional nose-high stall.

Another reason the shortcut fails is wind. A gust changes the relative wind. A sudden vertical gust can change the angle of attack even if the pilot has not moved the controls. That does not mean every gust causes a stall; it means the wing responds to relative wind, not simply the horizon.

**LEARNER:**

Is that why “keep your eyes outside” and “listen for the airplane” are part of stall awareness, instead of just watching the airspeed indicator?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. The ACS specifically expects recognition of impending and full-stall indications using sight, sound, or feel. The exact cues vary by airplane. You may have a stall-warning horn, light, stick shaker, buffet, less-responsive controls, changing wind noise, a changing sight picture, or a combination. Some airplanes have more warning than others. Some have equipment that others do not. Learn the cues and limitations of the airplane you are actually flying.

That last point is important. A stall warning device is a valuable cue, not a license to wait until it activates. The ACS explicitly calls out the range and limitations of stall-warning indicators as a risk-management item. Training should build recognition before the situation becomes a surprise.

**LEARNER:**

So I should not treat the horn as the definition of a stall.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. The horn, buffet, and other cues tell you something about proximity or condition; the aerodynamic definition is still critical angle of attack. The specific device and its behavior are part of the aircraft's equipment and documentation.

## [09:30] Why load factor changes the picture

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Now let's add load factor. Load factor is the ratio of the aerodynamic load the airplane is carrying to its weight. In straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight, the load factor is one G. In a level turn, the wing has to produce not only the lift that opposes weight but also a horizontal component that turns the airplane. To keep altitude while banked, the total lift must increase. Increasing total lift usually means increasing angle of attack, unless some other change supplies what is needed.

**LEARNER:**

And that is where the phrase “an airplane stalls at a higher airspeed in a turn” comes from.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes, with an important refinement: it is the increased load factor, not merely seeing a particular bank angle on the attitude indicator, that raises the stall speed. A steeper, coordinated level turn generally requires more load factor. But altitude, airspeed, pitch changes, turbulence, and maneuvering all affect what the airplane is actually experiencing. Do not reduce this to a magic-bank-angle rule.

Here is the practical thought process. When you bank and pull to hold altitude, you are asking the wing for more lift. The margin between the current angle of attack and the critical angle can become smaller. If you then pull more abruptly, encounter a gust, or make an uncoordinated control input, the margin can disappear faster than the airspeed number alone would suggest.

**LEARNER:**

Is that an accelerated stall?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

An accelerated stall is a stall occurring at a higher-than-normal airspeed because the airplane is under an increased load factor. The word “accelerated” here does not mean the airplane has to be gaining speed. It refers to acceleration or load. A level turn, a pull-up, a steep maneuver, or an abrupt control input can increase load factor. The wing still stalls for the same basic reason: critical angle of attack is exceeded.

**LEARNER:**

Then the bad sequence is not simply “bank equals stall.” It is more like: bank, pull, ask the wing for more lift, reduce the margin, and then add something like a gust or a skid.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is a much better model. It also explains why a pilot can be surprised. The airplane may be moving faster than the pilot's remembered published stall speed, while the wing is nonetheless being pushed to critical angle of attack by the total demand placed on it.

The ACS lists accelerated stalls as a risk item under power-off stall work, and it makes the same overall aerodynamic relationship part of both power-off and power-on stall knowledge. For the checkride, do not just recite “load factor increases stall speed.” Be ready to explain why, and to connect it to an actual flight situation.

## [13:30] Weight, center of gravity, configuration, power, and yaw

**LEARNER:**

The ACS groups a lot of things together: weight, center of gravity, power, attitude, and yaw. Do I need a separate explanation for every item?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

You need to understand the relationship, not memorize an unrelated list. Start with weight. A heavier airplane needs more lift to maintain the same flight condition than a lighter one. Other things equal, that can require a higher angle of attack and can change the airspeed associated with the critical angle. But do not take that as permission to invent a correction factor. Use the performance and limitation information for the aircraft.

Center of gravity affects stability, control forces, and stall characteristics. A particular airplane's approved documents describe its allowable center-of-gravity range and any operating limitations. We are not going to generalize a recovery or loading conclusion from one airplane to another. The important study point is that CG is part of the stall-characteristics picture, which is why the ACS includes it.

**LEARNER:**

And configuration means flaps, gear, speedbrakes, or whatever the airplane has.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Configuration changes the wing and the airplane's drag and lift characteristics. It changes the picture enough that the ACS requires knowledge across various airplane configurations, and the FAA repeatedly points pilots back to the specific airplane's POH or AFM for the approved procedures.

Power has two roles that people often confuse. First, power changes thrust and therefore the flight condition. At a high power setting, propeller effects and pitch forces can be very relevant. Second, adding power may be part of a manufacturer's recovery procedure and can reduce altitude loss. But power by itself does not remove the stall. If the wing remains above critical angle of attack, it remains stalled. The FAA's general stall-recovery discussion says the reduction of angle of attack is imperative; the actual aircraft procedure controls.

**LEARNER:**

So “power-on stall” does not mean the wing is somehow protected from a stall because the engine is making power.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. A power-on stall describes a practice or flight condition with power applied, often one that resembles a takeoff, departure, or go-around situation. The ACS expects the applicant to understand factors and situations that can lead to both power-on and power-off stalls. High power, a climbing attitude, a turn, distraction, and poor coordination can be a particularly demanding combination.

Now yaw. Yaw is rotation around the vertical axis, and it is a central part of spin awareness. At or near a stall, if the airplane is yawed, one wing can be more deeply stalled than the other. That difference can create autorotation. The FAA describes a spin as involving a stalled condition with yaw, and the ACS expects you to understand the relationship between yaw effects and spin aerodynamics.

**LEARNER:**

That makes coordination more than a cosmetic “ball in the middle” item.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Very much so. Coordinated flight matters throughout normal operations, and it becomes especially important when angle-of-attack margin is small. The goal is not to stare at one instrument and ignore the airplane. It is to develop a coordinated control habit, recognize when the airplane is asking for more than the situation safely allows, and avoid adding yaw when you are already close to a stall.

## [18:30] Recognizing an impending stall

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Let's make this more practical. Recognition is not one light, one sound, or one number. In a given airplane, an impending stall may announce itself through decreasing control responsiveness, aerodynamic buffet, a stall-warning system, a change in sound, a high deck angle, a high descent rate, or a sight picture that tells you the airplane is not doing what you intended.

**LEARNER:**

But you just said attitude is not enough.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

It is not enough by itself. It is still one piece of a larger cross-check. In a landing approach, for example, you are monitoring alignment, descent path, airspeed, power, configuration, coordination, and the feel of the airplane. If the approach starts to deteriorate, the answer is not to fixate on a single number. It is to recognize the unstable trend early and choose a safer path.

The FAA notes that power-off stall cues can be less noticeable than power-on cues in some airplanes. That is exactly why a pilot needs to learn the specific airplane and why normal approach discipline matters. A quiet, low-energy, high-workload moment near the ground is not where you want the first hint of a stall to be a surprise.

**LEARNER:**

Is “stall warning during normal operations” something the ACS specifically calls out because the warning is not automatically a checkride failure or a disaster, but it needs a correct response?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is a good way to frame it. The ACS includes stall warnings during normal operations as a risk-management consideration. The lesson is not to pretend a warning cannot happen. The lesson is to recognize it, understand what it is telling you, and avoid letting the condition develop into a loss of control.

One careful distinction: do not use this audio to decide how to react in a particular airplane. The required response, limitations, and procedure belong in the approved documents and your training. ## [21:30] The aerodynamic priority in recovery

**LEARNER:**

This is where the instinct can be strongest. If I feel the airplane stall, I want to pull because I do not want to lose altitude.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That instinct is understandable, and it is exactly why the core principle needs to be so clear. Pulling increases angle of attack. If the wing is already stalled, increasing angle of attack does not make it fly again. The immediate aerodynamic priority is to reduce angle of attack below critical.

The FAA's Airplane Flying Handbook presents a general recovery framework for light general aviation airplanes, but it also says the pilot must follow the aircraft-specific manufacturer's recommended procedures when they are published and current. The ACS likewise requires stall recovery in accordance with the procedures in the POH or AFM. So for this episode, retain the principle: first unstall the wing by reducing angle of attack. Then use the approved procedure to manage bank, coordination, power, configuration, and return to the desired flightpath.

**LEARNER:**

So the sequence of priorities matters. Trying to level the wings before reducing angle of attack can be the wrong first move because the wing is still stalled.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. The FAA notes that roll control can improve after the wing is flying again. It also warns against trying to fix a wing drop with aileron before reducing angle of attack, because the control input can aggravate the stalled condition. The same principle applies to the desire to hold altitude: altitude loss is unpleasant, but attempting to preserve it by continuing to pull can make the loss of control worse.

**LEARNER:**

And that is how a secondary stall becomes possible: recovering, then pulling again before the airplane has enough margin.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. A secondary stall is a risk item in the ACS. After an initial recovery, a pilot who immediately makes a large pull to regain altitude can exceed critical angle of attack again. The lesson is not a memorized control sequence; it is disciplined energy and angle-of-attack management, following the approved aircraft procedure.

**LEARNER:**

Where does the “power as needed” idea fit without turning it into “power first”?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Power can be important to reduce altitude loss or support the desired flightpath, and a particular airplane's published recovery procedure tells you how to apply it. But the FAA is explicit that power does not eliminate a stall. Reducing angle of attack is the aerodynamic action that removes the stalled condition. Keep those two ideas separate.

## [25:00] Scenario one: overshooting base to final

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Let's work through a common scenario. You are turning from base to final. The wind or your timing means you are going to overshoot the extended centerline. What is the tempting but dangerous response?

**LEARNER:**

Keep banking, maybe add inside rudder so the airplane comes around faster, and use opposite aileron to prevent the bank from getting too steep.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the classic cross-control trap. The airplane is turning, slow compared with cruise, close to the ground, and the pilot is focused on making the runway. Inside rudder can yaw the airplane. Opposite aileron can change the wing loading and angle-of-attack picture. If the pilot also pulls to keep the nose from dropping, the conditions for a cross-control stall and spin risk can build quickly.

Notice the decision error underneath it: treating runway alignment as more important than aircraft control. The runway is not going anywhere. An unstable turn is information. The safer decision may be to discontinue the approach using the procedure appropriate to the aircraft and situation rather than trying to force the airplane back to the centerline.

**LEARNER:**

So I should not summarize the lesson as “never bank in the pattern.” Normal turns are normal. The lesson is do not keep increasing demand on the wing and add yaw just because I do not want to go around.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. The ACS identifies cross-control stalls and inadvertent stall, spin, and loss-of-control factors as explicit risks. It wants you to understand why this can happen, not merely repeat a slogan. In a checkride conversation, connect the pieces: low altitude reduces recovery margin; turning and pulling can increase load factor; poor coordination adds yaw; a stall with yaw can progress toward a spin; and a go-around is a normal risk-management option when the approach is no longer stable.

We are not prescribing a particular go-around technique here. Use the approved procedure for your airplane and the guidance of your instructor. The study point is the decision to stop trying to salvage a deteriorating alignment through cross-controlled, high-angle-of-attack maneuvering.

## [31:00] Spin awareness: prevention first

**LEARNER:**

Let's make the spin distinction explicit. Is every stall a spin?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

No. A stall is a wing exceeding critical angle of attack. A spin is a more specific stalled and yawed condition involving autorotation. The FAA emphasizes that at least part of the wing is stalled in a spin and that yaw is central to the entry. That is why stall awareness and coordination are so closely linked.

**LEARNER:**

And a spiral dive is different because the airplane is not stalled, even though the situation can still be very dangerous.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. Do not call every steep, descending turn a spin. A spiral dive is an unstalled, tightening descent in which airspeed and load can increase rapidly. The recovery considerations are different. The important study habit is to identify the actual condition rather than react to a label.

For private-pilot spin awareness, the most valuable emphasis is prevention. Avoid getting slow, high-angle-of-attack, uncoordinated, distracted, and low all at once. Recognize the warning cues. Maintain directional control. Do not let an unstable pattern turn or departure become a contest of will.

**LEARNER:**

What about intentional spins for practice?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Do not assume they are authorized or appropriate. Intentional-spin approval, limitations, loading, and recovery procedures are aircraft-specific. FAA guidance directs pilots to the appropriate aircraft documents, and 14 CFR 91.9 requires compliance with the operating limitations in the approved flight manual, markings, and placards for the aircraft. This episode does not teach spin entry or recovery. If training involves intentional spins, that is a separate, aircraft-specific, instructor-led decision using the approved materials.

**LEARNER:**

So on an oral exam, I should know the conceptual recovery priority and I should say that the POH or AFM controls the aircraft-specific procedure.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. You should be able to explain the cause, the phases the ACS names, and the importance of the manufacturer's procedure. More importantly, you should show that you understand how to avoid ever needing that knowledge close to the ground.

## [40:00] ACS review and takeaways

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Let's close with five things worth retaining.

First: a stall occurs when critical angle of attack is exceeded. It is not defined by one airspeed, one pitch attitude, one sound, or one warning light.

Second: published stall speed is useful, but it belongs to stated conditions. Load factor, coordination, configuration, weight, center of gravity, power, and yaw can change the airspeed at which the critical angle is reached.

Third: recognize trends. Decreasing control responsiveness, buffet, a stall warning, changing sound, a degrading approach, or a demanding climb can all be cues to reassess rather than keep pulling.

Fourth: the immediate aerodynamic priority in an impending or full stall is reducing angle of attack. Then follow the approved, aircraft-specific procedure to manage the rest of the recovery. Do not make an audio lesson your checklist.

Fifth: a spin is a stalled, yawed condition. The prevention habit is coordinated control, early recognition, and the willingness to abandon a deteriorating approach or climb plan before it becomes a loss-of-control problem.
