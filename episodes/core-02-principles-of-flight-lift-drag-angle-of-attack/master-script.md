## [02:30] Start with the air

**ANNOUNCER:**

Start with the air:

**LEARNER:**

We talk about air as if it is empty space. But it is the thing the wing works on.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. The PHAK treats air as a fluid. It has viscosity, friction, and pressure. You do not need to become a fluid-dynamics engineer to use that idea. You do need to stop imagining the wing as gripping empty space.

As the airplane moves, the wing meets air. From the airplane’s point of view, that flow approaches from a direction opposite the airplane’s path through the air. We call that the relative wind. It is not necessarily horizontal, and it is not necessarily the same as the wind reported at an airport. It is the airflow the moving airfoil experiences.

An airfoil is a shaped surface designed to interact with airflow. A wing is the airfoil we will mostly mean in this episode. The horizontal tail and propeller blades are airfoils too, but keeping the wing in view will make the first picture easier to hold.

**LEARNER:**

So if I am descending, the relative wind is not level with the horizon.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. That distinction is the doorway to angle of attack. Before we get there, picture an airfoil with a leading edge, a trailing edge, and an imaginary straight line from one to the other. That imaginary line is the chord line.

## [04:00] Relative wind is a moving reference

**ANNOUNCER:**

Relative wind is a moving reference:

**LEARNER:**

If relative wind is an invisible line, how am I supposed to build a useful picture of it while I am learning to fly?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Start with the airplane’s flight path through the air, not its path over the ground. If an airplane is moving forward through still air, the relative wind approaches from directly ahead, opposite the flight path. If the airplane is climbing through the air, the relative wind approaches from somewhat below the airplane’s path. If it is descending, the relative wind approaches from somewhat above that path. The important word is “through the air.” A headwind or tailwind changes groundspeed, but it does not by itself change the airplane’s angle of attack in steady flight.

That distinction helps prevent a common category error. Wind reported at the airport is a movement of the whole air mass over the ground. Relative wind is the airflow caused by the airplane moving through that air mass. Both matter to a pilot, but they answer different questions. Airport wind helps with runway selection, takeoff and landing performance, and drift. Relative wind helps describe the aerodynamic forces on the airfoil.

**LEARNER:**

So a strong headwind can make the ground move by slowly while the wing still sees plenty of airflow.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. And a strong tailwind can make the ground move by quickly while the wing still sees the same airflow for the same indicated aerodynamic condition. We will not turn this into an instrument lesson, but it is worth separating true airspeed, indicated airspeed, groundspeed, and wind early. They are related measurements and conditions, not interchangeable names for one thing.

Use a two-line sketch. First, draw a side view of the wing: rounded leading edge in front, narrower trailing edge behind. Draw a straight line from its leading edge to its trailing edge. That is the chord line. It is fixed in the wing—if the wing rotates, that line rotates with it.

Now add one arrow. Make it point toward the wing from the direction opposite the airplane’s path through the air. That is the relative wind. If the airplane’s flight path changes into a climb or descent, that arrow changes direction even if the wing itself does not rotate. The small opening between the chord line and the approaching relative-wind arrow is the angle of attack. The horizon does not enter that picture at all.

You do not need to perfect the image from words alone. The show notes include labeled FAA diagrams for airfoil geometry, angle of attack, and pressure distribution; use them as you listen.

**LEARNER:**

Including why a climb can have a nose-high attitude without the wing necessarily being near a stall.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. A familiar visual attitude can be useful as part of learning a specific maneuver in a specific airplane. It is never a replacement for the underlying aerodynamic relationship.

## [06:20] The angle that matters

**ANNOUNCER:**

The angle that matters:

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Angle of attack, often shortened to AOA, is the acute angle between the chord line and the direction of the relative wind. Notice what is absent from that definition: the horizon.

**LEARNER:**

But in the airplane, I see pitch attitude. If the nose is high, isn’t the angle of attack high?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Often, a change in pitch changes angle of attack. But pitch attitude and angle of attack are not interchangeable labels. Pitch attitude compares the airplane to the horizon. Angle of attack compares the wing to relative wind. Change the flight path and the relative-wind direction changes too. A particular nose picture does not, by itself, tell you the angle of attack.

That is why a stall is not fundamentally a “low-speed event.” A wing reaches its critical angle of attack when its angle relative to the relative wind becomes too great. We will discuss stalls in more depth in a later podcast. For now, preserve the definition. Airspeed can be evidence about the airplane’s energy state and the wing’s likely margin, but it is not a direct substitute for knowing angle of attack.

**LEARNER:**

Then why do the airspeed markings and published stall speeds matter if angle of attack is what defines the stall?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

They matter because they are practical reference values for a specific airplane under stated conditions. A published one-G stall speed, for example, describes the speed associated with the critical angle of attack in the configuration, weight, and coordinated, unaccelerated condition used for that value. It gives the pilot a useful reference; it is not a promise that the wing has the same angle-of-attack margin in every other condition.

Change the demand on the wing and the airspeed associated with critical angle can change. A turn can increase load factor. Weight and configuration matter. Turbulence and abrupt control inputs can change the relative-wind picture. We will discuss load factor in more depth in a later podcast. For the airplane being flown, its POH or AFM helps connect these relationships to the approved speeds, configurations, and procedures for that specific aircraft.

The practical takeaway is not “ignore airspeed.” It is “use airspeed correctly.” Published values guide normal operation; the angle-of-attack model explains why the same number cannot answer every aerodynamic question by itself.

## [08:30] Changing angle of attack changes the conversation

**ANNOUNCER:**

Changing angle of attack changes the conversation:

**LEARNER:**

If I increase angle of attack, does lift always increase?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Up to a limit, increasing angle of attack changes the airflow and pressure distribution in a way that increases the wing’s lift-producing capability. But “increase angle of attack” is not a permanent command to make unlimited lift. There is a critical angle beyond which the smooth airflow behavior needed for the airfoil’s normal lift production breaks down. That is the aerodynamic boundary behind the stall discussion that comes later.

This gives us a key takeaway for the whole series: one of a pilot’s primary aerodynamic jobs is to manage the conditions that preserve useful airflow over the airfoil. Understanding why angle of attack, configuration, coordination, power, and the airplane’s approved procedures all matter is the foundation for that skill.

For this episode, notice the practical chain of reasoning. In steady, level flight, the wing needs to produce lift equal to the airplane’s weight. If the airplane becomes heavier, the wing needs more lift to hold the same flight condition. If air density is lower, or the airplane is moving more slowly through the air, the wing has less of the favorable airflow condition available for a given angle. A pilot can change some of those relationships with power and pitch, but cannot repeal the relationships.

**LEARNER:**

That sounds like the lift equation, but I do not want to get trapped in algebra before I understand the idea.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the right instinct. The equation is a compact way of saying that lift is related to air density, the square of velocity, wing area, and a lift coefficient. The lift coefficient is a way to summarize how the airfoil is working at a particular angle of attack and configuration. The equation does not make the airplane fly. It helps us describe why the same wing does not make the same amount of lift in every condition.

Use the lift equation as a checklist of influences, not as a chant. Density matters. Speed through the air matters strongly. Wing area matters. The airfoil’s condition and angle of attack matter. That is enough for a first pass.

**LEARNER:**

And when we say the square of velocity matters, we mean a modest change in airspeed can have a bigger-than-intuitive aerodynamic effect.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes, while holding the other factors fixed for the thought experiment. Real flying often changes several factors at once, which is why a pilot needs to reason from the whole condition rather than from one equation term.

## [10:50] What the wing changes

**ANNOUNCER:**

What the wing changes:

**LEARNER:**

I learned that the curved top of the wing makes air speed up, pressure drops, and that is lift. Is that wrong?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

It is incomplete. Pressure differences are real and important, but the PHAK explicitly cautions that lift is more complex than simple differential pressure between the top and bottom of a wing. Both Bernoulli’s principle and Newton’s laws operate whenever an airfoil is generating lift.

At an appropriate angle of attack, airflow around the airfoil creates a pressure distribution: generally lower pressure above and higher pressure below. At the same time, the airfoil changes the airflow’s direction. Air leaving the wing is deflected downward and backward. Newton’s third law describes the reaction: an upward and forward force on the airfoil.

The useful study habit is not to pick a team—Bernoulli or Newton. Ask what the airfoil and the surrounding airflow are doing. The wing’s shape, its angle to the relative wind, the air density, the wing area, and the speed through the air all matter to the amount of lift produced.

**LEARNER:**

And that is why a symmetrical airfoil can still make lift. It does not need a dramatically curved top.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. That is one reason the “longer path, same arrival time” story is such a poor foundation. The PHAK notes that lifting airfoils need not have a longer upper surface. What matters is the complete airflow and pressure picture at the angle of attack being flown.

## [13:10] Pressure is not a vacuum cleaner

**ANNOUNCER:**

Pressure is not a vacuum cleaner:

**LEARNER:**

When people say low pressure above the wing “pulls” it upward, is that a useful shortcut?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

It can become misleading if it makes you picture a vacuum cleaner over the wing. Pressure is a force distributed over a surface. Around a lifting airfoil, the pressure on the upper surface is commonly lower than the surrounding pressure, while pressure on the lower surface contributes a positive upward force. The distribution of those pressures creates a net aerodynamic force.

But the pressure picture and the flow-direction picture are not competing explanations. They describe the same aerodynamic event from different angles. The wing’s geometry and angle of attack shape the pressure field. The resulting flow leaves the airfoil with a downward and backward component. The PHAK uses both Bernoulli’s principle and Newton’s laws because both help account for observable parts of the event.

**LEARNER:**

So I should not imagine separate “Bernoulli lift” and “Newton lift” that add up like two engines.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. They are not separate mechanisms taking turns. They are complementary ways to reason about the pressure and momentum changes in the airflow. The safer study answer is: an airfoil at an appropriate angle of attack creates a pressure distribution and deflects air downward; together those descriptions explain the lift-producing aerodynamic force.

There is another reason not to lean on a shape-only story. Airfoils come in many shapes. Some are cambered, with a visibly different upper and lower contour. Some are symmetrical. A symmetrical airfoil can still generate lift when it meets relative wind at an appropriate angle of attack. That single fact should make us cautious about saying that a curved upper surface, by itself, is the reason a wing flies.

**LEARNER:**

And it means the pilot has a meaningful influence through the angle between the wing and the relative wind.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Not unlimited control, and not a substitute for understanding the airplane’s approved limits, but a central aerodynamic control relationship.

## [15:30] The total aerodynamic force

**ANNOUNCER:**

The total aerodynamic force:

**LEARNER:**

We draw lift straight up and drag straight back. Are those arrows literally attached to the wing that way?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

They are useful bookkeeping arrows, but begin with the relative wind. Aerodynamic force acts on the airfoil because of the airflow. For study purposes, we resolve that force into a component perpendicular to the relative wind—lift—and a component parallel and opposite to the relative wind—drag. That is why lift is not always vertical relative to the horizon, and drag is not always horizontal relative to the horizon.

In straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight, the familiar diagram is especially convenient: lift balances weight and thrust balances drag. But that is a particular condition, not the definition of the forces. In a climb, descent, or turn, their directions and balances must be considered relative to the flight path and the acceleration involved.

**LEARNER:**

Then the familiar textbook arrow is condition-specific—not wrong, but especially simple in a straight-and-level diagram.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. “Up” needs a reference. The FAA’s aerodynamic definition uses the relative wind. If you keep that reference, the drawing makes more sense in more situations. It also explains why induced drag exists. The aerodynamic force that provides a useful lift component is not always aligned perfectly opposite gravity. Its rearward component is induced drag.

This is a conceptual lesson, not a vector-calculation exercise. You do not need to calculate the components in your head. You do need to know that the clean four-force diagram is a model of a specified condition, and that lift and drag are named with respect to relative wind.

## [17:35] Two kinds of drag

**ANNOUNCER:**

Lift is only half the bargain. Now, the drag tradeoff.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Drag is the aerodynamic force that resists the airplane’s motion through the air. For this discussion, separate it into parasite drag and induced drag.

Parasite drag is the cost of moving the airplane’s surfaces through the air: the fuselage, landing gear, antennas, struts, and the skin friction of the airplane itself. As speed increases, parasite drag increases.

Induced drag is different. It is associated with making lift. A wing with a pressure difference between its lower and upper surfaces also creates wingtip vortices and a tilted aerodynamic force. The drag component of that force is induced drag. It is greatest when the wing is working at high angle of attack and low airspeed.

**LEARNER:**

So slow means induced drag is high, and fast means parasite drag is high?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

As a basic picture, yes. That is the tradeoff. At the slow end, the wing generally needs more angle of attack to make the lift required, and induced drag is large. At the fast end, parasite drag becomes large. Somewhere between those extremes is a region of lowest total drag. Drag never disappears; the mix changes.

Do not turn that idea into an aircraft-specific speed from memory. The precise numbers depend on the airplane, weight, configuration, and conditions. But the shape of the tradeoff explains why flying slower does not automatically save power and why faster does not always mean more efficient.

## [19:30] Parasite drag: the cost of moving the airplane through air

**ANNOUNCER:**

Let’s slow down the first half of the drag picture.

**LEARNER:**

Parasite drag sounds like an insult. What should I actually remember about it?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Remember that it is drag not directly created by the wing’s effort to make lift. It includes form drag from the shape of the airplane’s parts, interference drag where airflow around different parts interacts, and skin-friction drag from air moving across the surfaces. An airplane has a fuselage, landing gear or fairings, control-surface gaps, antennas, struts on some designs, and many other exposed shapes. Each affects the airflow.

The central relationship is that parasite drag increases as airspeed increases. As the airplane goes faster through the air, it has to spend more energy overcoming that resistance. This is why an airplane that appears clean and streamlined still needs more thrust as speed rises through much of its operating range.

**LEARNER:**

Does that mean a pilot should always keep the airplane slow to reduce parasite drag?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

No, because that would ignore induced drag. Aerodynamics rewards complete sentences. At a lower airspeed, the wing usually needs to operate at a higher angle of attack to produce the lift required for the flight condition. That raises induced drag. Parasite drag may be lower there, but total drag can be higher.

Treat the word “parasite” as a category label, not a judgment about a particular component. Some of the components that create parasite drag are essential. Landing gear lets the airplane operate from runways. Antennas make communication and navigation possible. The useful question is not whether a component is bad; it is how the airplane’s design and configuration affect the airflow and, therefore, performance.

**LEARNER:**

That also explains why the airplane’s configuration belongs in performance planning.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Configuration is one reason a memorized aerodynamic answer can become misleading. The pilot must use the approved information for the airplane being flown, especially when configuration, speeds, or performance figures are involved.

## [21:45] Induced drag: the cost that comes with lift

**ANNOUNCER:**

Now, the other half of the drag picture.

**LEARNER:**

If induced drag comes with lift, should I think of it as a penalty for having wings?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Think of it as a consequence of the three-dimensional wing doing its job. A wing makes lift by creating a pressure difference and changing the airflow. Near the wingtip, air tends to curl around from the higher-pressure region below toward the lower-pressure region above. The resulting vortex pattern and downwash affect the direction of the local airflow around the wing. The aerodynamic force is tilted enough to include a rearward component. That rearward component is induced drag.

The PHAK’s study relationship is clear: induced drag is greatest at high angles of attack and low airspeeds. This does not mean low airspeed is inherently wrong. Airplanes routinely operate at slower speeds during phases of flight where that is appropriate. It means the energy and power picture changes, and the pilot must understand the condition rather than treating every speed change as free.

**LEARNER:**

So when the wing must work harder to maintain the needed lift at a slower speed, it creates more induced drag.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the essential connection. It is also why “just pull a little more” is not a complete response to a changing energy condition. Pulling changes angle of attack; it can raise the lift coefficient, but it also changes drag and moves the wing closer to its critical angle. In actual flight, follow the aircraft-specific procedure and CFI instruction. As a study principle, understand the tradeoff before you try to memorize any maneuver response.

The same topic also prepares you for wake-turbulence study later. The wingtip-vortex idea is a reminder that the air disturbed by an airplane does not instantly become undisturbed at the trailing edge.

## [24:00] Lowest total drag is a balance point, not a magic number

**ANNOUNCER:**

Bring the two drag categories back together.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Imagine two curves. One represents parasite drag. It is relatively low at lower airspeeds and rises as speed rises. The other represents induced drag. It is high at lower airspeeds and falls as speed rises, within the normal range of the model. Add the two curves together and the sum has a low region between the extremes.

That low region represents a balance, not one universal airspeed to carry from airplane to airplane. As weight, configuration, and flight conditions change, the airspeed associated with that balance can change too. The POH or AFM for the airplane you plan to fly helps connect those relationships to that airplane’s specific speeds, configurations, performance information, and procedures.

The conceptual picture still matters. Below that low region, the induced-drag side of the tradeoff becomes more important; above it, the parasite-drag side becomes more important. That is why the power required and the airplane’s performance do not change in a straight line as airspeed changes.

**LEARNER:**

Is that related to the phrase “best lift-to-drag ratio”?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Lift-to-drag ratio compares how much useful lift the wing is producing with how much drag accompanies it. The PHAK treats that ratio as an important measure of aerodynamic efficiency. At the best ratio for a particular configuration and condition, the airplane gets the most lift for each unit of drag. We will meet the operational consequences of that idea in later performance and emergency discussions. Here, use it to reinforce the central tradeoff: the airplane has no single “drag setting.” It has a changing balance of drag sources.

**LEARNER:**

And that is why the phrase “more speed is more drag” can be true but still incomplete.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Precisely. More speed increases parasite drag. But a low-speed condition can demand a higher angle of attack and carry substantial induced drag. A good answer names which drag is changing and why.

## [26:00] Connect the forces to power and flight path

**ANNOUNCER:**

Connect the forces to power and flight path:

**LEARNER:**

When the airplane slows in level flight, why does it sometimes feel like I need more power even though I am going slower?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Because power is used to overcome drag and to support changes in the airplane’s energy state. At lower speeds, the induced-drag portion of total drag can rise. Holding altitude while slowing may require a different combination of pitch and power than a learner expects from automobile intuition, where lower speed often simply means less resistance.

In an airplane, altitude is also energy. A climb, descent, acceleration, or deceleration changes the energy picture. The four-force diagram is still useful, but do not force it to say that every force is perfectly balanced in every condition. If the airplane accelerates, decelerates, climbs, descends, or turns, the force relationships are changing in ways that deserve careful, aircraft-specific instruction.

**LEARNER:**

So the word “power” is not a synonym for speed, and pitch is not a synonym for altitude.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Aerodynamically, power affects thrust and energy available. Pitch changes the wing’s relationship to the relative wind and the flight path. Airspeed, altitude, configuration, weight, and load factor all remain part of the condition.

The ACS identifies angle of attack, airspeed, load factor, power setting, weight and center of gravity, attitude, and yaw as an interrelated set in its slow-flight knowledge item. That is the relationship we have been building: a change in one part of the condition can affect the others. It reminds a practical-test applicant and a working pilot to connect the variables rather than treating one as the answer to every question.

## [28:15] A relationship, not a single gauge

**ANNOUNCER:**

Put the pieces together with a simple scenario.

**LEARNER:**

Suppose I am trying to maintain altitude and the airplane slows. What is the basic aerodynamic problem?

**INSTRUCTOR:**

If other relevant factors stayed the same, a lower speed means the wing has less dynamic pressure available. To continue producing the lift needed for level flight, the airplane will generally need a higher coefficient of lift, commonly achieved by increasing angle of attack. That change also tends to increase induced drag.

But this is where pilots get in trouble if they make the story too simple. The ACS relationship includes power, weight, load factor, attitude, and yaw, not just airspeed and angle of attack. A turn changes the load requirement. A change in weight changes the lift required. Power changes the energy picture. Yaw affects the airflow each wing experiences. The airplane is a system, not an airspeed needle with wings attached.

**LEARNER:**

So the point of the foundation is to explain why the later lessons have several variables.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. In flight training, your CFI will connect those variables to a specific airplane and its approved procedures. This episode gives you the language to understand that explanation and ask better questions.

## [34:30] How to study this chapter productively

**ANNOUNCER:**

How to study this chapter

**INSTRUCTOR:**

After listening, draw a simple airfoil. Mark its chord line. Draw relative wind approaching from the opposite direction of the flight path. Then label angle of attack between those lines. Next, draw the aerodynamic force and resolve it into lift perpendicular to relative wind and drag opposite relative wind. Finally, write one sentence for each drag category: parasite drag rises with speed; induced drag is associated with lift and is greatest at high angle of attack and low speed.

Do not judge your sketch by its artistic quality. Its purpose is to make the reference directions visible. If you can explain the drawing aloud without using “up” or “back” until you have named the reference line, you are building a useful habit.

Try a second check without looking at the notes. Ask yourself four questions. What is the reference direction for lift? What two lines define angle of attack? Why is a wing’s top-surface curvature not a complete explanation of lift? Why can an airplane have substantial drag at a slow speed? If you cannot answer one of them in a few clear sentences, return to the relevant PHAK section and redraw the airflow picture.

Then make the questions slightly harder. Imagine the same airplane at a different weight, a different configuration, or in a turn. Do not try to calculate anything from this episode. Instead, identify which variables now need attention. Weight changes the lift required. Configuration can change the airfoil and drag picture. A turn brings load factor into the relationship. This is the kind of disciplined expansion that keeps a foundation useful instead of rigid.

One final self-check: say the words “relative wind” before “angle of attack,” and say the words “which drag?” before making a statement about speed. Those two pauses prevent many memorized answers from becoming inaccurate explanations.

If a diagram seems to contradict the words, return to the reference directions. Draw the relative wind first. Place lift perpendicular to it and drag opposite it. Then ask whether the drawing represents straight-and-level flight, a climb, a descent, or a turn. Naming the condition is part of explaining the forces correctly.

**LEARNER:**

Relative wind is the airflow direction the moving airfoil experiences. Angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and that relative wind, not the angle between the airplane and the horizon. Lift is not explained by one slogan: pressure differences and the wing’s downward deflection of air are both part of the picture. Parasite drag grows as speed increases, while induced drag is associated with lift and is greatest at high angle of attack and low airspeed.
