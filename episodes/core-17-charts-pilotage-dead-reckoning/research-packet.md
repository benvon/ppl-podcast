# Charts, Pilotage, and Dead Reckoning — research packet

## Scope

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners preparing to connect sectional interpretation with basic visual navigation.
- Target duration: 30–45 minutes, approximately 4,200–6,300 spoken words.
- Core question: How does a VFR pilot turn a current sectional into a route picture, use pilotage and dead-reckoning predictions together, and recognize when actual progress no longer matches the plan?
- Scenario: A completely fictional 72-nautical-mile daytime VFR route from Cedar Junction Airport to Pine Lake Airport. The route, airports, landmarks, airspace, terrain, obstruction elevations, winds, headings, and times are teaching data and do not describe a real flight.
- Out of scope: Aircraft-specific power settings or procedures; selecting a legal or prudent cruise altitude for an actual flight; fuel and diversion planning; VOR, GPS, and radar navigation; lost procedures; real airport instructions; radio calls; and step-by-step in-flight corrective maneuvering. Episode 16 supplies the airspace operating-requirement framework, Episode 18 will cover navigation systems, and Episode 19 will cover integrated cross-country and fuel planning.

## Scenario calculation audit

- Route distances: 12 + 18 + 16 + 14 + 12 = 72 nautical miles.
- Planned true airspeed: 105 knots. Fictional wind: from 330 degrees at 15 knots. A wind-triangle calculation gives a true heading of about 067 degrees and groundspeed of about 108 knots for a true course of 075 degrees.
- Fictional charted variation: 5 degrees west. Applying west variation to true heading gives magnetic heading 072 degrees.
- Fictional aircraft deviation card: for magnetic 072, steer compass heading 074.
- Planned leg times at 108 knots: 12 NM = 6 minutes 40 seconds; 18 NM = 10 minutes; 16 NM = 8 minutes 53 seconds; 14 NM = 7 minutes 47 seconds; 12 NM = 6 minutes 40 seconds. Total = 40 minutes.
- Observed first-leg groundspeed: 12 NM in 8 minutes = 90 knots.
- If the next 18-NM leg also takes 12 minutes, the second checkpoint is reached 20 minutes after departure and the observed trend remains 90 knots.
- Remaining distance after 30 NM: 42 NM. At 90 knots, estimated remaining time is 28 minutes, producing a revised 48-minute total and an eight-minute-later destination estimate than the original 40-minute plan.

## Likely trouble spots

- Misconception: A sectional is a complete, permanent picture of current conditions.
  - Correction: Check the chart's effective dates and current NOTAM information; treat the chart as one current planning source, not a frozen guarantee.
  - Source: Chart Users' Guide, p. 6.
- Misconception: A Maximum Elevation Figure is a prescribed or automatically safe cruising altitude.
  - Correction: It is a quadrant elevation figure derived from terrain and obstacle data with specified allowances and limitations. Actual altitude selection must account for the route, regulations, weather, aircraft performance, and other current information.
  - Source: Chart Users' Guide, pp. 12–13; ACS PA.I.D.K2.
- Misconception: Any charted road, town, or tower makes a good checkpoint.
  - Correction: Prefer prominent features with a distinctive relationship to other features; do not rely on one point.
  - Source: PHAK Ch. 16, p. 16-12.
- Misconception: Course, heading, and track are interchangeable.
  - Correction: Course is the desired ground path, heading is where the nose points, and track is the actual path over the ground.
  - Source: PHAK Ch. 16, pp. 16-9–16-10.
- Misconception: The wind correction calculation eliminates the need to look outside and verify position.
  - Correction: Dead-reckoning predictions are monitored and corrected through pilotage at checkpoints.
  - Source: PHAK Ch. 16, p. 16-13.
- Misconception: A late checkpoint proves the wind forecast was wrong.
  - Correction: Delay can reflect wind, climb, timing, identification, tracking, or another mismatch. Confirm position and collect corroborating evidence before assigning a cause.
  - Source: Teaching synthesis from PHAK Ch. 16, pp. 16-12–16-13 and 16-21.
- Misconception: Fixing navigation means spending more time head-down.
  - Correction: The ACS explicitly treats distraction, task prioritization, loss of situational awareness, and collision hazard as risks. PHAK workload guidance calls for preparing anticipated work while workload is low, and AC 90-48E warns that fixation inside can draw attention away from the outside environment. A prepared log, brief entries, and deferred nonurgent arithmetic preserve the outside visual scan.
  - Source: FAA-S-ACS-6C, PA.VI.A.R1-R2, PDF viewer p. 46; PHAK Ch. 2, p. 2-24; AC 90-48E, section 15.

## Editorial risks to avoid

- Turning the chart section into an inventory of symbols instead of asking what each feature changes about the route picture.
- Using a fictional obstacle, airspace label, or airport as if it appeared on a real chart.
- Repeating airspace entry and equipment rules already taught in Episode 16, *Airspace, Equipment, and VFR Weather Minimums*.
- Treating a sectional or MEF as the complete altitude-selection answer.
- Using course, heading, track, magnetic heading, or compass heading before defining the relevant term.
- Letting the Learner diagnose a wind error before position and timing evidence establish a mismatch.
- Giving a step-by-step in-flight correction maneuver rather than the decision framework and a CFI practice bridge.
- Adding GPS, VOR, fuel, diversion, lost-procedure, or real-airport instructions that belong to later episodes.
