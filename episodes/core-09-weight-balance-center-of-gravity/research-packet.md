# Weight, Balance, and Center of Gravity — research packet

## Scope

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners preparing for ground study, flight planning, and the practical test.
- Target duration: approximately 42 minutes; 30–45 minute editorial window.
- Core question: How does a pilot turn the actual airplane, occupants, baggage, cargo, and fuel into a loading decision that remains within weight and CG limits throughout the flight and feeds a realistic performance plan?
- Primary anchors: FAA-S-ACS-6C PA.I.F, Performance and Limitations, and FAA-H-8083-25C Chapter 10, Weight and Balance.
- Supporting anchors: FAA-S-ACS-6C PA.II.B cargo securement and current 14 CFR § 91.9 operating-limitations and approved-manual requirements.
- Out of scope: aircraft weighing procedures; mechanic-level empty-weight determination; transport-category average-weight programs; aircraft-specific arms, limits, fuel-use sequences, performance numbers, or correction procedures; detailed percent-MAC computations; ballast installation; and regulatory endpoints that do not change the ordinary private-pilot planning decision.

## Source-led teaching chain

1. **Decision frame:** The ACS requires a valid computation, correction of out-of-CG loading, all-phase limit compliance, and use of performance data.
2. **Separate limit questions:** PHAK Chapter 10 distinguishes total weight from the location of CG and explains that either can fail while the other passes.
3. **Mental model:** A seesaw and fulcrum make the turning effect visible before datum, arm, and moment transfer that lever model to the airplane.
4. **Aircraft-specific foundation:** Current and accurate weight-and-balance records and the AFM's loading charts, graphs, or manufacturer procedures form the calculation foundation. Section 91.9 supplies the operating-limitation boundary.
5. **Calculation:** List the airplane and loads, obtain and total weights and moments, recognize a moment index as a simplified moment value, calculate or plot CG, and compare both coordinates with the limits. A spoken pointer sends listeners to the labeled FAA computational worksheet in the show notes.
6. **Handling effects:** Forward CG increases control demand and can reduce low-speed nose-up authority; aft CG reduces longitudinal stability and stall/spin recovery capability.
7. **Performance connection:** Weight increases required lift and can degrade takeoff, climb, cruise, stall, approach, and landing performance; approved performance data completes the decision.
8. **Across-flight connection:** Fuel burn removes weight at the fuel tanks. PHAK notes that tanks in most small aircraft are near CG, so CG generally changes little, but the actual airplane's planned fuel conditions still require the all-phase check.
9. **Correction:** Shift, add, or remove weight; then rerun loading, fuel, and performance planning rather than treating one acceptable output as the end of the process.
10. **Physical integrity:** Secure each item in the station used by the calculation.

## Likely trouble spots

- Misconception: Below maximum gross weight means the airplane is properly loaded.
  - Correction: Total weight and CG are independent comparisons; either can be out of limits while the other is acceptable.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, pp. 10-1 and 10-7.
- Misconception: A sample PHAK or POH problem supplies usable data for every airplane of that model.
  - Correction: Samples demonstrate a method; the actual airplane's current empty-weight, equipment, arms, limits, and approved method control.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, pp. 10-4 and 10-6; 14 CFR § 91.9.
- Misconception: Moment is another word for weight.
  - Correction: Moment combines weight with arm, so the same weight has a different effect at a different station.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, pp. 10-5–10-6.
- Misconception: Any point that appears below maximum weight on an envelope is acceptable.
  - Correction: The plotted point has both a weight and a moment or CG coordinate and must be inside every applicable boundary.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, Figure 10-8, p. 10-8.
- Misconception: Fuel burn always improves weight and balance.
  - Correction: It reduces total weight. PHAK says tanks in most small aircraft are near CG, so CG generally changes little, but the actual airplane's planned fuel conditions still require checking.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, p. 10-11.
- Misconception: Moving baggage forward corrects an overweight condition.
  - Correction: Moving an item changes moment and CG but leaves total weight unchanged.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, p. 10-10.
- Misconception: Reducing fuel is a complete loading correction.
  - Correction: The revised fuel must still support the route, requirements, reserves, risk margin, and performance plan.
  - Source: PHAK Chapter 10, p. 10-2.
- Misconception: A correct worksheet remains correct if baggage can move.
  - Correction: The ACS separately requires securing items and cargo; physical restraint preserves the assumed station.
  - Source: FAA-S-ACS-6C PA.II.B.K4 and S1, p. 13.

## Illustrative-model boundaries

- Spoken examples use only direction-of-change reasoning and generic worksheet rows.
- No generic arm, fuel density, C G limit, maximum weight, or performance number is presented for operational reuse.
- PHAK Figures 10-2 through 10-4 are used only to teach datum, arm, and moment.
- PHAK Figures 10-7 and 10-8 are used only to teach the graph-to-envelope workflow.
- Every operational calculation is redirected to the current POH/AFM, supplements, markings, placards, equipment record, and current weight-and-balance data for the intended airplane.

## Editorial risks to avoid

- Turning the terminology section into a glossary recital instead of a lever-based explanation.
- Treating maximum gross weight as a performance guarantee.
- Suggesting that an approved-envelope boundary is a target rather than a limit.
- Claiming that fuel burn moves CG in one universal direction.
- Letting a Learner summary introduce a concept before the Instructor establishes it.
- Reading sample numerical procedures that sound transferable to another airplane.
- Repeating the aircraft-specific boundary after every concept instead of defining it once and applying it naturally.
- Using slogans such as “weight is what you carry; balance is where you carry it” in place of the two full decision questions.

## Open technical questions

None. Detailed part 23 certification endpoints, mechanic weighing procedures, percent-MAC computations, and ballast procedures were removed from scope because they are not required to answer the episode's typical private-pilot planning question.
