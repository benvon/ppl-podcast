## [02:31] Stall: name the condition first

**ANNOUNCER:**

Stall: name the condition first.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

A stall is an aerodynamic condition. It occurs when the wing exceeds its critical angle of attack and the smooth airflow over the wing is disrupted.

Build the picture one piece at a time. Imagine looking at the wing from its side. Draw a straight line from the rounded leading edge to the trailing edge. That imaginary line is the chord line. It is fixed to the wing; it moves wherever the wing moves.

Now draw an arrow approaching the wing from ahead and a little below or above, depending on the wing’s path through the air. That arrow represents relative wind. Unlike the chord line, its direction changes when the airplane’s path through the air changes.

Angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and that relative-wind arrow. Increase that angle far enough, and the airflow can no longer stay smooth over the wing in the same way. The critical angle of attack is the point where the stall occurs.

Notice what is not in that definition: a particular indicated airspeed, a particular horizon picture, or a particular power setting. FAA guidance says the critical angle of attack can be exceeded at any airspeed, any attitude, and any power setting.

That does not make airspeed, attitude, and power irrelevant. It means they are clues and conditions, not the definition. They help us understand how close we may be to the critical angle of attack in a particular situation.

If you can see diagrams while listening, the show notes link directly to the Airplane Flying Handbook’s critical-angle-of-attack figure. Look for the chord line, the relative-wind arrow, and the angle between them. Those are the pieces to keep separate in your mind.

**LEARNER:**

I think I see why “the wing stalls at this speed” is incomplete. The speed can be associated with a situation, but the wing reaches the stall because the angle between the wing and relative wind became too large.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the earned conclusion. Say it in plain language: a stall is about angle of attack. Airspeed often warns us about a changing condition, but it does not redefine the condition.

## [04:51] Recognize the approach to the limit

**ANNOUNCER:**

Recognize the approach to the limit.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Angle of attack is not usually shown by a standard cockpit needle. That is why stall recognition matters. The Airplane Flying Handbook describes an impending stall as a condition in which the angle of attack has produced a warning but has not reached the critical angle. A full stall follows when the critical angle is exceeded.

The useful word here is recognition, not prediction by one cue. Depending on the airplane and equipment, the pilot may notice buffet, an aural alert, a light, a stick shaker, a changing sound of airflow, changing control feel, a slower airplane response, or a visual picture that deserves attention. The exact combination is not universal.

Some airplanes have stall-warning devices; some provide warning through their aerodynamic behavior; some have both. A stall horn is valuable information, but it is not a complete substitute for understanding the airplane or for noticing the rest of the condition.

The practical bridge is your POH or AFM. It tells you what warning equipment and indications your airplane has, what its approved procedures are, and what speeds and configurations the manufacturer publishes. General aerodynamics tells you why those things belong together. The approved documents explain how they apply to the airplane you are planning to fly.

Yes. The general takeaway is to preserve useful airflow over the airfoil by recognizing a developing high-angle-of-attack condition early. The airplane-specific cues and procedures come from the airplane’s approved information and training.

## [06:36] Airspeed is useful, but it is not the definition

**ANNOUNCER:**

Airspeed is useful, but it is not the definition.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Published stall speeds are not dismissed by the angle-of-attack conversation. They become more understandable.

The Airplane Flying Handbook explains that a published level-flight, one-G stall speed is valid only under particular conditions: unaccelerated one-G flight, coordinated flight, one stated weight—typically maximum gross weight—and a particular center of gravity—typically maximum forward CG.

Picture that as a label on a carefully described test condition, not as a floating line that follows the airplane through every possible flight condition. The number is useful because it tells you something specific about that airplane under those stated conditions. It is not a universal statement that the wing is always equally far from critical angle of attack whenever the airspeed indicator shows a particular value.

What can change? Weight can change. Center of gravity can change. Configuration can change. The airplane can be turning or accelerating. Coordination can change. The wing may be asked to produce a different aerodynamic force.

The POH or AFM is where those general relationships meet the particular airplane: its published speeds, the conditions attached to them, its configurations, limitations, and procedures. That is why we should not replace a published number with a slogan about angle of attack. We need both levels of understanding.

**LEARNER:**

So the practical use is not “ignore the published speed because stalls are about angle of attack.” It is “use the published speed for the conditions it describes, and remember that changed conditions can change the airspeed where the same critical angle is reached.”

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. That bridge keeps the theory from becoming vague and keeps the number from becoming magical.

## [08:20] Load factor: a way to name wing demand

**ANNOUNCER:**

Load factor: a way to name wing demand.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Load factor is a compact name for how much aerodynamic load the wing is supporting compared with the airplane’s weight. The word *factor* just means a multiplier.

At one G, the wing is supporting one airplane-weight’s worth of load. That is the familiar, unaccelerated baseline: think straight flight with no change in the path that is pressing you unusually hard into the seat. At two G, the wing is supporting twice the airplane’s weight in aerodynamic load, and you feel about twice the normal seat pressure.

That does **not** mean the airplane’s actual mass has doubled. It means that, for that moment and condition, the wing has been asked to produce twice as much supporting aerodynamic force as the one-G baseline. That distinction is why the phrase is useful. It names the job the wing is doing, not a property permanently attached to the airplane.

**LEARNER:**

So one G is my reference point, and two G means “the wing is carrying twice the usual aerodynamic load,” not “this airplane has somehow become twice as heavy.”

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. And because the wing has a critical angle of attack, extra demand matters. In a given condition, one way to make more aerodynamic force is to increase angle of attack. That can move the wing closer to its critical angle even though the airspeed may look higher than the published one-G stall speed.

Here is the practical takeaway before we look at a turn: load factor is the bridge between what you feel, what the wing is being asked to do, and why the stall-speed picture can change.

## [10:03] How a level turn makes the demand visible

**ANNOUNCER:**

How a level turn makes the demand visible.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

This is one of the places where a diagram is genuinely worth a look. The show notes link to the PHAK figures. But you can form a workable mental picture without one.

Imagine one arrow coming out of the wing. In straight-and-level flight, draw that arrow straight up. Its upward part supports the airplane against gravity.

Now begin a coordinated turn while maintaining altitude. The wing-force arrow tilts inward, toward the center of the turn. The inward part is what bends the flightpath. But once the arrow tilts, a shorter share of it points upward. If you did nothing else, there would no longer be enough upward support to maintain altitude.

So, to keep altitude, the whole arrow has to grow. Its upward part still needs to equal the airplane’s weight, and it now also needs an inward part to make the turn. That larger whole arrow is the increased load factor.

Notice the conditions in that picture: *coordinated* and *constant altitude*. It is not a claim that any bank angle, in every climb, descent, slip, or skid, automatically produces one memorized G value. It is a precise picture of why a level turn creates added wing demand.

The FAA’s PHAK gives a useful checkpoint: in a coordinated, constant-altitude turn at 60 degrees of bank, the load factor is 2 G. The wing must produce twice the airplane’s weight in aerodynamic load to maintain altitude while also curving the path.

**LEARNER:**

So the extra G in that picture is not caused by the bank angle alone. It comes from trying to hold altitude after the wing’s supporting force has been tilted inward.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the important articulation. Bank changes the direction of the force. Holding altitude makes the wing increase the size of the force. Together, in a coordinated level turn, they create the familiar load-factor relationship.

And this is why the FAA warns that the relationship gets more demanding quickly as bank increases. A little more bank at the steep end is not a little more work for the wing. It can be substantially more work.

## [12:19] Why G changes the stall-speed picture

**ANNOUNCER:**

Why G changes the stall-speed picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Return to the wing’s limit. The wing does not know or care whether the extra demand came from a level turn, a pull-up, or another maneuver that increases load. It still has to make the required aerodynamic force without exceeding critical angle of attack.

If the wing is asked to carry more load, it needs more angle of attack to support that load in the otherwise comparable condition. That is the connection between load factor and accelerated stalls: the critical angle can be reached at a higher airspeed than the published one-G value.

The PHAK describes the numerical relationship this way: stall speed changes with the square root of load factor. We are not using that as a cockpit calculation. It is a compact way to see the size of the change. At two G, the multiplier is the square root of two—about 1.41. As a simple illustration, a relevant one-G stall speed of 50 knots becomes about 71 knots at two G, with the comparable conditions assumed.

That is not a speed to carry into a maneuver. It is not a replacement for a POH or AFM number. It is proof that the phrase “I have plenty of airspeed” can be incomplete when the wing is being asked for substantially more load.

The reverse mistake is also worth avoiding. A higher indicated airspeed does not, by itself, tell you the current load factor or angle of attack. Load factor describes the aerodynamic demand in the condition; angle of attack describes how close the wing is to its limit. The variables are related, but they are not interchangeable labels.

**LEARNER:**

So I should not memorize “sixty degrees means a particular stall speed.” I should recognize the level-turn example as two G, understand why two G moves the stall-speed relationship, and use my airplane’s published information for the actual condition.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. That is the useful three-part connection: **a changed flightpath can add wing demand; added wing demand can require more angle of attack; and the same critical angle can then arrive at a higher airspeed.**

**ANNOUNCER:**

Bank angle is a clue, not the whole answer.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

It is tempting to turn the 60-degree, two-G example into a shortcut: see a bank angle, announce a load factor, and move on. But the conditions are part of the idea.

In the force-arrow picture, the demand grew because the airplane was both banked **and maintaining altitude**. The vertical part of the tilted force still had to support the airplane, so the whole force had to become larger. ## [18:18] Make the square-root relationship useful

**ANNOUNCER:**

Make the square-root relationship useful.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Let’s slow down over the square-root statement, because it is easy to hear as a formula and miss its meaning.

The one-G published stall speed is the baseline for the relevant airplane and stated condition. If the load factor doubles from one G to two G, the stall speed does **not** double. It changes by the square root of two—about 1.41 times the one-G value. If the load factor rises to four G, the square root is two, so the comparable stall speed is twice the one-G value.

The numbers matter only as an illustration of scale. They explain why increasing load can move the stall condition to a much higher airspeed, even though the wing still stalls for the same underlying reason: it reached critical angle of attack.

Try saying the idea without the arithmetic: “The harder I ask the wing to support and turn the airplane, the less useful it is to treat the familiar one-G stall speed as the whole picture.” That is the operationally relevant thought. The POH or AFM supplies the actual approved numbers, limitations, and procedures for the airplane.

## [19:57] Feel, instruments, and limits

**ANNOUNCER:**

Feel, instruments, and limits.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Load factor often has a human cue: increasing pressure into the seat. That cue can help the idea become intuitive. At two G, the airplane and its occupants have twice the effective weight in the FAA’s level-turn example, so the seat feels different.

But seat pressure is not a precision instrument and it is not a stall warning. The PHAK notes that experienced pilots can often judge load from the feel of seat pressure, while also noting that an accelerometer is the instrument that measures load factor. In many general-aviation airplanes, that is not an everyday cockpit display.

So use the feeling as a prompt for the larger scan, not as a conclusion. Ask what the airplane is doing. Is the path curving? Is altitude being maintained? Is the wing near a high-angle-of-attack condition? Is the airplane coordinated? What does the POH or AFM say about the configuration and the applicable limitations?

This is also where two different safety ideas meet. A higher load factor can reduce aerodynamic margin before a stall, and it is also structural load on the airplane. Neither concern cancels the other. The airplane-specific operating limitations exist because the approved envelope has both aerodynamic and structural boundaries.

## [21:38] Using the idea without turning it into a rule of thumb

**ANNOUNCER:**

Using the idea without turning it into a rule of thumb.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Load factor matters in two connected ways. Aerodynamically, it can narrow the margin before critical angle of attack. Structurally, it is also the load the airplane must support. That is why the idea belongs beside—not in place of—the POH or AFM.

Your POH or AFM gives the airplane-specific published speeds, operating limitations, and procedures. Those details account for the airplane, its approved configurations, and the conditions the manufacturer used. The general model tells you why a number or limit matters; the approved information tells you what applies to the airplane you are flying.

In practice, use load factor as an early-awareness tool, not as a number to chase. When you are asking the airplane to change its path while holding altitude—especially as the turn steepens—or when you feel an increasing pull in the seat, recognize that the wing may be working harder. Avoid trying to rescue a developing situation with abrupt, escalating control pressure. Protect the airflow over the airfoil with smooth, coordinated control and make the lower-demand decision early when the situation calls for it.

That does not produce a universal procedure from a podcast. It gives you a question to bring to training and to the POH or AFM: “In this airplane and this configuration, what loads and margins am I creating as I ask it to change its path?”

## [23:36] Coordination changes the risk picture

**ANNOUNCER:**

Coordination changes the risk picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Coordination is not cosmetic. It changes how airflow reaches the wings and how a developing stall can unfold.

Yaw is rotation around the airplane’s vertical axis: the nose moving left or right relative to the path. A coordinated airplane has the appropriate relationship among bank, turn, and yaw for the condition. An uncoordinated airplane is slipping or skidding, and the relative airflow is no longer arriving symmetrically at the wings.

Imagine two wings that are supposed to be sharing the aerodynamic work. If yaw changes the airflow each wing receives, one wing can approach its critical angle differently from the other. That matters especially close to the stall, where small differences can produce a roll or become part of a spin entry.

The ACS calls out uncoordinated flight as a risk item. The Airplane Flying Handbook’s turning-stall discussion makes the consequence concrete: in a slip, the outer wing may stall first and move down abruptly; in a skid, the bank can increase to a potentially dangerous attitude.

This is not an invitation to diagnose every slip or skid from a podcast. It is a reason to treat coordination as a primary protection for the airflow over the airfoil, especially when the wing is already being asked to work hard.

**LEARNER:**

So coordination matters here because yaw can make the two wings see different airflow when the margin is already small.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. That is the causal connection. A high angle of attack is one part of the problem. Add yaw, and the airplane may no longer be asking both wings to do the same aerodynamic job.

## [25:21] The aerodynamic priority in recovery

**ANNOUNCER:**

The aerodynamic priority in recovery.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The sequence matters because a stall is an angle-of-attack condition. FAA guidance describes reducing angle of attack until the stall warning is eliminated, then leveling the wings, maintaining coordination, and applying power as needed.

That order is not just a list to recite. Reducing angle of attack addresses the condition that made the wing stall. Trying first to hold the nose at a preferred horizon picture, regain altitude immediately, or force a turn can leave the wing at too high an angle of attack. That is why the ACS includes secondary stalls as a risk item.

There is an important boundary here. The ACS says the stall recovery is performed in accordance with the POH or AFM. The manufacturer’s procedure and limitations control for the particular airplane. This episode explains the aerodynamic reason that reducing angle of attack comes first; it does not replace the approved procedure or training in that airplane.

**LEARNER:**

That makes “reduce angle of attack first” easier to understand. It is not a slogan about pushing the nose. It is addressing the airflow condition before trying to resume the desired flightpath.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Well put. The control movement, configuration, power, and other details depend on the airplane and procedure. The aerodynamic priority is to stop exceeding the critical angle of attack.

## [26:45] A pattern decision, not a trick turn

**ANNOUNCER:**

A pattern decision, not a trick turn.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Now apply the chain to a bounded pattern picture. Imagine that the airplane is turning from base toward final and the runway alignment is not working out as expected. The key decision is not how to salvage the turn with more rudder. The key decision is to stop trying to force the airplane through a picture that is developing into an overshoot.

FAA guidance identifies a classic risk chain: a pilot attempts to use rudder to bring the nose around rather than accept the overshoot and use the appropriate alternative. That can create a skid. If the wing is already at a high angle of attack, the stalled-and-yawed condition can lead toward a spin.

Notice the causal chain, not the drama. A turn can increase wing demand. A pilot may try to tighten the result. Yaw is added through uncoordinated control. The wing may be close to critical angle of attack. Those are connected hazards, not separate events.

The prevention lesson is deliberately plain: do not try to rescue runway alignment by creating a skid. Maintain coordination and make the safe decision appropriate to the situation, using the procedures and training for the airplane and operation.

## [28:19] Spin avoidance starts earlier

**ANNOUNCER:**

Spin avoidance starts earlier.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

A spin is not merely a steep turn or a sudden roll. The ACS asks private-pilot learners to understand what causes a spin, its entry, incipient, and developed phases, and the relevant recovery procedure.

For avoidance, begin earlier. FAA guidance identifies exceeding critical angle of attack while applying excessive or insufficient rudder as the primary cause of an inadvertent spin. In other words, a stalled wing and uncoordinated yaw belong in the same warning sentence.

An incipient spin begins when the airplane stalls and rotation starts. A developed spin is a more stabilized rotating condition. Those terms help describe what is happening; they should not tempt a listener to treat spin entry as something to explore without appropriate aircraft approval, instruction, altitude, and planning.

The airplane-specific boundary matters here even more strongly. The POH or AFM and the airplane’s limitations determine what is approved and what recovery procedure applies. The general safety lesson is not a generic spin-recovery mnemonic. It is to avoid creating the stalled-and-yawed combination in the first place, and to recognize a developing high-angle-of-attack condition before it becomes a full stall.

**LEARNER:**

So spin avoidance is not a separate topic after stalls. It is the reason coordination and timely angle-of-attack management matter so much near a stall.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Protect useful airflow, stay coordinated, and do not keep demanding a flightpath that requires the wing to work beyond its available margin.

## [29:51] Retrieval review

**ANNOUNCER:**

Retrieval review.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Here is the whole lesson in five short connections.

First: a stall occurs when angle of attack exceeds the critical angle. A particular airspeed is not the definition.

Second: published one-G stall speeds are useful because they describe stated conditions. Your POH or AFM connects those conditions to the specific airplane’s speeds, configurations, limitations, and procedures.

Third: when the wing is asked for more aerodynamic force, as in a level turn, it may need a higher angle of attack. Added load factor can therefore move the associated stall airspeed upward.

Fourth: stall recognition is airplane-specific. Learn the cues available in the airplane you are flying, and use them as part of a wider awareness of the flight condition.

Fifth: avoid the chain that combines high angle of attack with yaw. Coordination is one of the pilot’s primary protections for airflow over the airfoil. Do not force an overshooting turn with rudder.

For a load-factor check, answer three questions in your own words. In a coordinated, constant-altitude turn, why does the wing need more total aerodynamic force? Why does the 60-degree, two-G example not automatically give you a number for every banked flight condition? And why can a two-G illustration move the stall-speed relationship upward without changing the definition of a stall?

The answers are: the wing’s force is tilted and must still provide vertical support; the level-turn conditions are part of the example; and the wing still stalls at critical angle of attack, but added load can require more angle of attack and reach that limit at a higher airspeed. If those answers feel connected rather than memorized, you have the core load-factor idea.
