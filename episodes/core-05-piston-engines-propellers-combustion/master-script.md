## [00:00] Opening

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Move the throttle in a piston airplane and several things seem to happen at once. The engine note changes. The tachometer moves. The propeller changes the air moving around the airplane. The airplane accelerates, climbs, or answers with a different control demand.

Inside the cowling, though, the chain is remarkably physical. A small quantity of fuel and air burns. Pressure pushes a piston a few inches. A connecting rod turns a crankshaft. The crankshaft turns a propeller. And that propeller—an airfoil spinning through the air—produces the thrust that moves the airplane.

This episode slows that chain down far enough to see every link, then puts it back together so the engine instruments, propeller behavior, and combustion warnings tell one connected story.

## [03:30] Pressure becomes rotation

**ANNOUNCER:**

Pressure becomes rotation.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

The word *reciprocating* means moving back and forth. In a reciprocating engine, the pistons travel back and forth—or, with the cylinders arranged horizontally, side to side—from one end of each cylinder to the other.

Picture one cylinder as a sturdy metal tube closed at the outer end by the cylinder head. A piston fits inside and can slide along the tube. Near the closed end are an intake valve, an exhaust valve, and, in a common spark-ignition engine, spark plugs. The intake valve admits a fresh charge. Here, *charge* simply means the air-and-fuel mixture that enters the cylinder to be compressed and burned. The exhaust valve provides a path for burned gases to leave. The spark plugs begin combustion at the intended time.

The piston is connected by a connecting rod to a crankshaft. The crankshaft is the part that makes back-and-forth motion useful as continuous rotation. A bicycle crank gives a rough mental picture. Your foot moves through a repeating path, but the offset crank turns that movement into rotation at the sprocket. In the engine, combustion pressure supplies the push, the piston and connecting rod transmit it, and the offset portion of the crankshaft turns.

**LEARNER:**

The piston does not push the airplane forward. It pushes the connecting rod, which turns the crankshaft. The propeller later turns that rotation into thrust.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. Keeping those energy conversions separate prevents several later confusions. Cylinder pressure is not thrust. Crankshaft rotation is not thrust. They are necessary steps on the way to thrust.

The PHAK describes two principal reciprocating-engine ignition designs. A spark-ignition engine compresses a premixed fuel-air charge and then uses a spark plug to start combustion. A compression-ignition engine first compresses air enough to raise its temperature, then injected fuel ignites in that hot compressed air. Many training airplanes use spark-ignition engines, so that is our main model today, but “piston engine” does not automatically mean “spark plug and aviation gasoline.”

The cylinder arrangement can vary too: radial, inline, V-type, or horizontally opposed. Horizontally opposed engines remain common in smaller airplanes, with cylinders extending from both sides of the crankcase.

Across those designs, the central chain remains recognizable: combustion creates pressure; pressure moves a piston; the connecting rod turns a crankshaft; and the crankshaft supplies rotation to the propeller.

**LEARNER:**

And that tells me why the POH’s engine description matters. It identifies which version of this general chain is actually installed—spark or compression ignition, direct drive or geared, fixed-pitch or constant-speed propeller, and the controls and instruments that go with it.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. The general model lets you ask better aircraft-specific questions.

## [06:18] Four strokes, one power event

**ANNOUNCER:**

Four strokes, one power event.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

A *stroke* is one full trip of the piston from one end of the cylinder to the other. The common spark-ignition four-stroke engine completes intake, compression, power, and exhaust in four piston strokes and two crankshaft revolutions.

The names are easiest to retain if you attach a valve position, a piston direction, and a purpose to each one. See the show notes for **“Four-stroke cycle diagram — PHAK Figure 7-5.”**

Start with intake. The intake valve opens, the piston travels away from the cylinder head, and the increasing cylinder volume draws in the fuel-air charge. Picture pulling back the plunger on a syringe while its tip is open. More interior volume creates the pressure difference that brings material inside. In the engine, the induction system supplies the charge, but the moving piston provides the drawing action.

Second is compression. The intake valve closes. Both valves are now closed, and the piston travels back toward the cylinder head. The same charge is squeezed into a smaller space. Pressure and temperature rise. Compressing the charge prepares it to release substantially more useful force when it burns.

Third is power. Near the end of compression, the spark plugs ignite the charge. Combustion produces a rapid, controlled rise in temperature and pressure. That pressure acts across the face of the piston and drives it away from the cylinder head. The connecting rod transfers the push to the crankshaft. This is the stroke that adds useful energy to crankshaft rotation.

Fourth is exhaust. The exhaust valve opens. The piston travels toward the cylinder head and pushes burned gases out of the cylinder. At the end of exhaust, the cycle is ready to admit a fresh charge and begin again.

**LEARNER:**

Intake brings a charge in. Compression prepares it. Power is the timed pressure event that turns the crankshaft. Exhaust clears the cylinder for the next charge.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the cycle. A useful hand memory is *in, squeeze, push, out.* It is not a replacement for the proper names; it is a way to retrieve the motion. Intake: in. Compression: squeeze. Power: push. Exhaust: out.

Now speed the picture up. Even at a modest engine rpm, this cycle repeats hundreds of times per minute in each cylinder. And the cylinders are not all on the same stroke together. In a four-cylinder engine, one cylinder may be taking in a charge while another compresses, another produces power, and another clears exhaust. Their power events are precisely timed so the crankshaft continues rotating rather than receiving one isolated shove followed by a long pause.

The rotating assembly carries the engine between individual power events while the staggered power strokes keep adding energy. What sounds like one continuous engine note is a rapid series of cylinder events.

**LEARNER:**

That helps me understand roughness. If the cylinders are supposed to contribute timed events to one continuous rotation, a cylinder that is not burning normally can make the sequence uneven enough to hear or feel.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Roughness is evidence that the events are not contributing as evenly as expected. It does not identify the cause by itself. Ignition, fuel-air distribution, a mechanical problem, or another condition can disturb the sequence. The right response comes from the airplane’s checklist and POH, but the four-stroke model explains why an uneven event can reach the pilot as vibration, sound, or lost power.

The engine depends on induction, fuel metering, ignition, lubrication, cooling, and exhaust to sustain the cycle. Episode 6 develops those supporting relationships. For now, keep the timing chain clear: admit, compress, burn at the intended time, clear, repeat.

## [10:05] The propeller is a rotating wing

**ANNOUNCER:**

The propeller is a rotating wing.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Once the crankshaft is rotating, the propeller has work to do. The PHAK calls the propeller a rotating airfoil. That is more than a resemblance. A propeller blade has an airfoil cross-section. It meets a relative wind at an angle of attack. It creates an aerodynamic force with useful and resisting components, and it can be affected by drag and stall just as other airfoils can.

For a propeller blade moving along a helical path, the useful component of its aerodynamic force acts forward as thrust. The resisting component creates the torque load the engine must overcome.

**LEARNER:**

So the engine does not simply fling air backward with a flat paddle. It rotates an airfoil, and that airfoil develops an aerodynamic force because of its shape, speed, and angle of attack.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. The PHAK identifies blade airfoil shape, blade angle of attack, and rpm as major variables in thrust. Forward airspeed belongs in the working picture too because it changes the direction and speed of the relative wind meeting the rotating blade.

Picture a point halfway out on one propeller blade. While the engine turns the propeller, that point travels around a circle. At the same time, the whole airplane carries the point forward through the air. The blade therefore does not meet air from only one direction. Its relative wind is the combination of rotational motion and forward motion.

At low forward speed with substantial rpm, rotation is the larger part of that combination. As the airplane accelerates, the forward component grows. If blade angle stays fixed, the changing relative wind changes the blade’s angle of attack. That is why a fixed-pitch propeller can be well matched to one combination of rpm and airspeed but less efficient at another.

To picture propeller load, compare pedaling a bicycle in low and high gear. The low gear turns quickly with less force per stroke; the high gear asks for more force. Propeller pitch is not literally a gear, but a blade angle that takes a larger aerodynamic “bite” places more resisting torque on the engine, while a smaller bite lets rpm rise more readily.

**LEARNER:**

And the propeller is where the mechanical and aerodynamic halves meet. The engine’s torque tries to accelerate the propeller. Aerodynamic resistance loads it. The observed rpm is where those effects settle for the current condition.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. That is a much stronger model than “throttle position equals rpm.” Throttle affects what the engine can produce. Propeller pitch, airspeed, air density, and installation determine the load opposing it. Rpm is the resulting rate of rotation, not a complete explanation by itself.

## [13:12] Why a propeller blade is twisted

**ANNOUNCER:**

Why a propeller blade is twisted.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Look closely at a propeller blade and its angle changes from the hub to the tip. That twist solves a problem created by geometry.

Picture two dots painted on the blade. Put one close to the hub and one close to the tip. In one propeller revolution, both dots go around exactly once in exactly the same time. But they do not travel the same distance. The dot near the hub traces a small circle. The dot near the tip traces a much larger circle. Because speed is distance divided by time, the tip dot must move much faster through the air.

The show notes link **“Propeller blade twist and local blade speed — PHAK Figures 7-6 and 7-7”** places those ideas side by side: blade angle changes along the span, and travel distance increases toward the tip.

Now add the airplane’s forward motion. Every blade section shares the same forward speed, but each has a different rotational speed. Near the hub, the rotational component is relatively slow compared with the forward component. Near the tip, the rotational component is much faster. Those different combinations mean that an untwisted blade would not meet the relative wind at the same useful angle of attack along its length.

The blade is therefore built with a larger blade angle near the hub and a smaller blade angle near the tip. *Blade angle* here means the angle between the blade’s chord line and the propeller’s plane of rotation. It is a geometric setting in the blade. *Blade angle of attack* is the angle between the chord line and the blade’s actual relative wind. The first is about blade geometry; the second depends on geometry plus the current rotational and forward airflow.

**LEARNER:**

That distinction is important. Twist gives each blade section a different built-in angle, but its actual angle of attack still changes with rpm and airspeed.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. Twist helps the different parts of the blade share the aerodynamic work more uniformly. It does not freeze one angle of attack into the propeller for all conditions.

The same geometry gives meaning to an rpm limit: rpm governs not only how often the engine cycles, but how fast every part of the propeller moves, with the tip moving fastest.

**LEARNER:**

So the twist is not decorative and not mainly about how the propeller looks when stopped. It is how one rotating blade accommodates a wide range of local speeds from hub to tip.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. And it leads naturally to the next question: if blade angle matters, can the pilot or the propeller system change it?

## [16:03] Fixed pitch means a designed compromise

**ANNOUNCER:**

Fixed pitch means a designed compromise.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

On a fixed-pitch propeller, the manufacturer sets the blade angle and it cannot change in flight. One unchanging angle cannot be ideal both for slow takeoff and climb and for faster cruise. The designer therefore chooses a tradeoff that favors the conditions the airplane is meant to serve, while accepting some loss of efficiency in the others. That makes the installation light, simple, and relatively inexpensive.

The PHAK describes climb and cruise fixed-pitch propellers. A climb propeller uses lower pitch. It presents less aerodynamic resistance, so the engine can turn a higher rpm and develop more of its available power for takeoff and climb. A cruise propeller uses higher pitch. It places more aerodynamic load on the engine, produces lower rpm for a comparable condition, and favors cruise efficiency while giving up some takeoff and climb performance.

Using the load comparison, climb pitch lets the engine turn faster in a low-airspeed condition; cruise pitch takes a larger aerodynamic bite better suited to higher-speed travel.

**LEARNER:**

Then two otherwise similar airplanes with different approved fixed-pitch propellers could show different static or takeoff rpm and different climb and cruise performance.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. The installed propeller is part of the airplane’s performance configuration. Use that airplane’s POH or AFM, limitations, and approved propeller information rather than importing an expected rpm from another installation.

In a typical fixed-pitch installation, the tachometer is the engine-power indicator. The throttle controls fuel-air flow to the engine. Opening the throttle lets the engine produce more torque, and rpm generally increases until engine torque and propeller load balance again.

But “rpm is the power indication” does not mean “rpm uniquely equals horsepower in every condition.” The PHAK gives an important example. At a higher altitude, air density is lower. An engine can show the same 2,300 rpm yet produce less horsepower than it would at sea level. Maintaining that rpm as altitude increases may require opening the throttle farther because the less-dense air limits the charge entering the cylinders and the power the engine can make.

Forward airspeed also affects fixed-pitch rpm. In a descent, rpm can rise without advancing the throttle because the changed airflow lets the propeller turn faster. The tachometer still needs monitoring when power is reduced.

**LEARNER:**

The tachometer tells me the actual rotational speed. In a fixed-pitch airplane that is the primary power indication, but altitude, air density, airspeed, and propeller installation determine how much power and thrust are associated with that rpm.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. The POH or AFM connects the general relationship to specific power settings and performance. The tachometer markings add operating information: a normal range, maximum rpm, and sometimes a restricted range that should not be used continuously. Those markings are not decoration; they are the limits for the installed engine-propeller combination.

## [19:14] Constant speed separates power from rpm

**ANNOUNCER:**

Constant speed separates power from rpm.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

A constant-speed propeller can change blade angle in flight. The propeller control selects the governor’s target rpm. The governor senses a tendency for rpm to change and adjusts blade angle to hold the selected rpm as airspeed or propeller load changes—so long as the propeller remains within its governing range. More blade angle adds aerodynamic load; less blade angle reduces it.

Think about cruise control holding road speed as terrain changes. A propeller governor instead holds rotational speed by changing blade angle. If rpm tends to rise because propeller load decreases or airspeed increases, it increases blade angle. If rpm tends to fall because load increases or airspeed decreases, it decreases blade angle.

**LEARNER:**

So a steady tachometer no longer proves that power is steady. The governor may be changing blade angle to preserve rpm while throttle setting, manifold pressure, airspeed, or propeller load changes.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. That is the key conceptual shift.

In the typical arrangement described by the PHAK, the airplane has a throttle and a propeller control. The throttle changes engine power by changing the intake restriction. The propeller control selects the governor’s rpm target. The manifold-pressure gauge supplies the other major indication. It measures absolute pressure in the intake manifold—the passage carrying air and fuel toward the cylinders.

At constant rpm and altitude, higher manifold pressure corresponds to more fuel-air charge delivered to the cylinders and more power. But manifold pressure is not a percentage-power gauge by itself. Altitude, temperature, rpm, mixture, induction design, and engine limitations affect the power relationship. The airplane’s published power-setting tables combine the variables for the installed engine and propeller.

With the engine stopped, the manifold-pressure gauge reads approximately ambient pressure. During an intake stroke, the descending piston increases cylinder volume, so pressure inside that cylinder falls below the pressure in the intake manifold. Air then moves into the cylinder because the pressure upstream is higher than the pressure in the expanding cylinder. The piston does not pull air in with a separate force called suction; it creates the lower cylinder pressure that establishes the pressure difference.

In a normally aspirated engine with the throttle partly closed, the throttle restricts the intake path, so manifold pressure is itself lower than ambient pressure. It is still higher than the pressure in the expanding cylinder during intake, so charge can flow into the cylinder. Opening the throttle reduces that restriction and raises manifold pressure toward ambient. That increases the pressure available upstream of the cylinder, allowing more charge to enter on each intake stroke. The fuel-metering system adds fuel with the airflow, while the mixture control determines the fuel-to-air ratio. Episode 6, *Fuel, Mixture, Carburetion, Oil, Cooling, and Electrical Systems*, will build that induction and mixture picture in depth.

**LEARNER:**

That makes “manifold pressure” less abstract. The piston creates the lower pressure in the cylinder; the throttle establishes how much pressure is available in the manifold upstream. A partly closed throttle lowers manifold pressure. Opening it raises manifold pressure and lets more charge enter.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. Now connect it back to rpm. A high manifold pressure asks the cylinders to produce strong power events. A low selected rpm means those strong events occur fewer times per minute and can place higher pressure load on each event. The PHAK therefore says there is a manifold pressure that should not be exceeded for a given rpm and directs the pilot to the manufacturer’s recommended power settings and control sequence.

That is precisely where general theory should stop and aircraft-specific information should take over. Different engines, propellers, governors, turbocharging systems, and operating approvals produce different limitations and procedures. Learn the model—throttle, manifold pressure, engine torque, governor, blade angle, rpm—then use the POH or AFM for the permitted combinations and the correct order of adjustment.

A constant-speed propeller also has physical blade-angle limits called pitch stops. Within the constant-speed range, the governor has room to change pitch and hold rpm. Once a blade reaches the low- or high-pitch stop, the governor cannot move it farther in that direction. Rpm can then change with airspeed or load much as it does with a fixed-pitch propeller.

**LEARNER:**

So “constant speed” means the system regulates toward selected rpm within a range. It does not mean rpm is mechanically guaranteed in every condition.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. Whenever a system name sounds absolute, look for its operating range and limits.

## [23:02] Combustion is a timed pressure event

**ANNOUNCER:**

Combustion is a timed pressure event.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Return inside the cylinder. Saying “the mixture burns” is accurate but incomplete. The engine needs the charge to burn in a controlled way and at the correct time so cylinder pressure rises when it can do useful work without opposing the piston or overstressing engine parts.

In normal spark-ignition combustion, the spark plugs start small flame fronts. Those flame fronts spread across the compressed charge. The pressure rise is rapid, but it is progressive and predictable rather than one instantaneous explosion of the entire charge. The expanding gases deliver their strongest useful push as the piston begins the power stroke.

**LEARNER:**

So combustion timing is really pressure timing. The engine does not only need heat. It needs pressure to build in the right part of the piston’s travel.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Too late, and useful pressure arrives after the best leverage and expansion opportunity have passed. Too early, and pressure can oppose a piston that is still moving toward the cylinder head. Too abrupt, and the pressure and temperature rise can damage the chamber, piston, valves, or cylinder.

Crankshaft position provides the mechanical clock. The ignition system times the spark so the spreading flame produces its useful pressure rise during the power stroke. The exact timing belongs to the engine design.

Now recall that multiple cylinders contribute power strokes in sequence. Correct ignition timing is therefore about more than one piston. It keeps the engine’s series of pressure events synchronized with crankshaft position. A mistimed or incomplete event can reduce power, add roughness, raise temperature, or increase mechanical stress.

The show notes include **“Normal versus explosive combustion — PHAK Figure 7-21.”** The figure contrasts a flame spreading across the chamber with an explosive event. Use it to attach a visual pattern to *normal combustion* and *detonation*.

**LEARNER:**

The normal model is now: spark, spreading flame, smooth pressure rise, useful push. That gives us something specific to compare with abnormal combustion.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Abnormal combustion is not simply “a hotter burn.” It changes when or how pressure develops.

## [25:25] Detonation is explosive combustion

**ANNOUNCER:**

Detonation is explosive combustion.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Detonation is uncontrolled, explosive ignition of the charge in the combustion chamber. It creates an abrupt, excessive rise in pressure and temperature.

People sometimes use *detonation* to mean any combustion problem or early ignition. The important distinction is how the charge burns: normal combustion spreads predictably from the spark plugs; detonation is uncontrolled and explosive.

The PHAK identifies high cylinder-head temperature as a characteristic indication and says detonation is most likely at high power. Less severe detonation can present as overheating, roughness, or lost power. Severe or sustained detonation can damage pistons, cylinders, and valves quickly because those parts receive pressure and heat beyond the designed pattern.

**LEARNER:**

From the cockpit, roughness or high temperature tells me the engine is not operating normally, but it does not let me watch the flame front and prove that detonation is the cause.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Right. The physical model helps you understand the risk. The aircraft’s indications and checklist guide the response.

The PHAK names several operating factors associated with detonation: fuel below the manufacturer’s specified grade; extremely high manifold pressure combined with low rpm; high power with an excessively lean mixture; and extended ground operation or steep climb that reduces cooling.

Each factor either makes the unburned charge more prone to auto-ignite or exposes the cylinder to greater heat and pressure. Incorrect fuel can have insufficient resistance to detonation. Excessive manifold pressure for the rpm raises cylinder demand. High power with an excessively lean mixture or poor cooling lets damaging heat accumulate.

This is also why the engine, propeller, fuel, mixture, and cooling topics cannot be completely isolated in real operation. We separate them into episodes so each model can be learned clearly, then reconnect them. Episode 6 will develop how mixture and cooling affect the combustion environment. Here the key point is that normal combustion depends on a controlled charge, controlled temperature and pressure, and the engine-propeller power relationship staying inside the airplane’s published boundaries.

**LEARNER:**

Then “avoid detonation” is not one magic control movement. It is an operating relationship: correct fuel, approved power combinations, appropriate mixture, adequate cooling, and attention to engine indications.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. And those relationships are specific enough that the POH or AFM and checklist should supply the actual settings and actions. If temperature, roughness, power, rpm, or manifold pressure does not fit the expected condition, treat the mismatch as information that deserves attention rather than waiting for one instrument to announce a diagnosis.

## [28:05] Preignition starts before the spark

**ANNOUNCER:**

Preignition starts before the spark.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Preignition is separated from detonation by timing. The fuel-air charge begins burning before the engine’s normal ignition event because something hot inside the combustion chamber ignites it.

A carbon deposit, a damaged spark plug, a cracked spark-plug insulator, or another overheated part can become that hot spot. The charge no longer waits for the correctly timed spark. Combustion begins while the piston is still traveling upward on compression.

Picture two people pushing on opposite sides of a door. During the power stroke, the expanding gas and piston motion work in the same direction: pressure pushes the piston down. With preignition, expanding gas can push down while the crankshaft is still driving the piston up. The engine must fight its own early pressure. Temperature rises, power falls, and the piston, cylinder, or valves can be damaged.

**LEARNER:**

So detonation changes the character of combustion after the spark has begun the event. Preignition changes the start time by lighting the charge before the intended spark.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the clean conceptual distinction.

Real engine indications may not keep the categories so clean. The PHAK explains that detonation and preignition can happen together and that one can cause the other. Detonation’s extreme heat can create a hot spot that begins preignition. Preignition can produce the temperature and pressure conditions that encourage detonation. Both can show up as high engine temperature and reduced performance, making them difficult to distinguish from the cockpit.

The distinction explains the damaging mechanisms. The pilot recognizes that the normal relationship has broken down and uses the approved abnormal procedure rather than trying to make a maintenance diagnosis in flight.

The PHAK closes the relationship with practical prevention: use the recommended fuel and operate within the engine’s proper temperature, pressure, and rpm ranges. That sentence connects the whole lesson. Fuel properties affect the charge. Combustion creates pressure. Rpm tells how fast the crankshaft and propeller are turning. Manifold pressure helps describe the engine’s load-producing condition. Temperature shows part of the thermal demand. Published ranges define how those variables are intended to coexist in the installed system.

**LEARNER:**

So the useful cockpit takeaway is not “I will decide whether this is detonation or preignition.” It is “I know why both are dangerous, I recognize abnormal heat or lost performance, and I follow the airplane’s guidance before the condition compounds.”

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Understand the mechanism; act on the available evidence and the approved procedure.

## [30:37] Two sparks, two independent sources

**ANNOUNCER:**

Two sparks, two independent sources.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Most standard certificated spark-ignition airplanes use dual ignition: two magnetos, two sets of high-tension leads, and two spark plugs in each cylinder.

A magneto is an engine-driven electrical generator dedicated to ignition. It uses a permanent magnet to create the high voltage needed for a spark to cross the spark-plug gap. Because the engine drives it, the magneto does not depend on the airplane’s battery or alternator once the crankshaft is turning.

The show notes link **“Dual-magneto ignition layout — PHAK Figure 7-16”** shows one magneto connected to one plug in each cylinder and the other magneto connected to the second plug.

Two independent sides provide redundancy. If one magneto fails, the other can continue firing one plug in each cylinder. The engine can continue operating, although the PHAK says a slight power decrease can be expected.

The second spark plug also improves normal combustion by starting flame fronts in two parts of the chamber. The normal rpm decrease during a magneto check reflects operation on one ignition source and one plug per cylinder rather than two.

**LEARNER:**

That explains why some rpm drop is expected when selecting one magneto. Combustion is still happening in every cylinder, but it is beginning from one spark location instead of two.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Correct. The before-takeoff ignition check compares operation on BOTH with operation on each magneto separately. The ACS asks learners to understand why checklist items are performed and how they help detect malfunctions. This check asks whether each independent ignition side can carry the engine and whether its effect falls within the airplane’s published limit.

If the engine stops when RIGHT is selected, the engine is not sustaining operation on the right magneto—the magneto selected to operate. The same logic applies to LEFT. A stop, excessive rpm drop, or roughness identifies an unacceptable ignition-check result; it does not by itself identify the failed component. The PHAK directs the pilot not to fly until the problem is corrected. No rpm drop can also be abnormal because it may indicate that the switch did not ground the deselected magneto. The permissible drops, maximum difference, engine speed, control setup, and required action come from the POH or AFM and checklist for the airplane.

**LEARNER:**

So the check is not a ritual of moving the switch through L and R. It is a controlled comparison: both ignition paths together, then each path alone, with a published expected result.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. A good systems check deliberately changes one part of the system and asks whether the rest of the indication changes as expected.

## [33:15] The propeller is always live

**ANNOUNCER:**

The propeller is always live.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Magneto independence creates a permanent safety implication. Turning the master switch off does not remove the magneto’s ability to make ignition energy. If the ignition switch is on and the propeller moves the crankshaft, the magneto can produce a spark and the engine can fire.

Even selecting the ignition switch OFF does not make a propeller safe to handle. The OFF position normally grounds the magnetos so they cannot fire. If a grounding wire is broken or disconnected, a magneto may remain live despite the switch position. Residual fuel in a cylinder and a moved propeller can then produce an unexpected power event.

**LEARNER:**

That changes the mental model from “the propeller is stopped” to “the engine is capable of being turned from outside.” The switch position is not enough evidence for me to treat the propeller like an inert object.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. Treat every propeller as capable of moving and every propeller arc as a hazard. Keep people and property clear. Do not use the master switch as proof that ignition is disabled.

The same principle runs backward through the whole power chain. During normal operation, the engine turns the propeller. But a moved propeller can also turn the crankshaft. Crankshaft motion can drive the magneto. The magneto can create a spark. The spark can ignite a charge. One link can energize the next in either direction.

**LEARNER:**

And a “hot prop” is not only a case where somebody forgot the ignition switch. A failed grounding path can create the same hazard even when the cockpit switch appears correct.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Yes. That is why the safe behavior is based on what the system can do, not on confidence that a switch or wire is perfect.

## [35:05] Read relationships, not isolated numbers

**ANNOUNCER:**

Read relationships, not isolated numbers.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Bring the lesson back to the instrument panel. The pilot does not see cylinder flame fronts or the governor moving blade angle. The pilot sees indirect evidence. The best use of that evidence is comparison: compare the indication with what the current configuration should produce, compare it with the previous condition, and compare related indications with one another.

Start with a fixed-pitch airplane. Increase throttle in level flight. The engine admits more charge, combustion events add more torque, and rpm generally rises. The propeller creates more thrust, and airspeed begins to change. As the airplane accelerates, the relative wind at the propeller changes too. A new balance appears among engine torque, aerodynamic propeller load, and rpm.

Now climb without a supercharger. Air density decreases. Even with the throttle opened farther, the engine may not receive the same mass of air it received near sea level. A familiar rpm can therefore represent less horsepower and less available thrust. The same tachometer number does not erase the density change.

Now picture a constant-speed airplane. Advance the throttle while the governor is controlling rpm. Manifold pressure can rise and power can increase while the tachometer remains nearly fixed, because the governor increases blade angle to absorb the added engine torque. If you looked at rpm alone, you would miss the power change. If you looked at manifold pressure alone, you would miss whether the rpm relationship is approved.

**LEARNER:**

In both airplanes the instruments make sense only when I know what the propeller system is doing. Fixed pitch lets rpm change with the torque-and-load balance. Constant speed changes blade angle to preserve selected rpm and makes manifold pressure part of the power picture.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly.

Next, add combustion health. A rising temperature, increasing roughness, or lost power means the pressure events are no longer producing the expected result. If rpm falls unexpectedly in a fixed-pitch airplane, ask what else changed: throttle position, airspeed, air density, fuel-air supply, ignition, temperature, sound, and smoothness. In a constant-speed airplane, the governor may temporarily hide lost power by reducing blade angle to hold rpm, while manifold pressure, airspeed, or thrust changes reveal the mismatch.

Normal system knowledge helps you recognize an abnormal condition sooner. The checklist supplies the response, the POH or AFM supplies limitations and instrument meaning, and maintenance personnel determine the underlying fault.

The ACS’s system-failure language is therefore connected to ordinary monitoring. A malfunction rarely introduces itself with the name of the broken part. It often appears first as a relationship that no longer makes sense: an rpm change without the expected control change; an engine sound that does not match the tachometer; manifold pressure that does not respond normally; unusual roughness; increasing temperature; or power that does not produce expected thrust and performance.

**LEARNER:**

The practical habit is to know what should move together, then notice when the group separates. Throttle, manifold pressure, rpm, temperature, sound, smoothness, airspeed, and thrust are different windows into the same power chain.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

That is the idea. And the POH or AFM makes it specific. Use its engine description to identify the installed system. Use its limitations to interpret colored arcs and red lines. Use its normal procedures and performance tables to learn approved power combinations. Use its abnormal and emergency procedures when the indications no longer fit.

A power setting learned in one airplane is not universal. Similar airplanes can have different engines, propellers, governors, instrument markings, or limitations. Transfer the reasoning, then verify the numbers.

## [38:21] Retrieval review

**ANNOUNCER:**

Retrieval review.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Five connections to retain.

First: a reciprocating engine converts chemical energy into pressure and heat, piston motion, and crankshaft rotation. The propeller converts that rotation into aerodynamic thrust.

Second: the four strokes are intake, compression, power, and exhaust. Intake admits the charge. Compression prepares it. Timed combustion creates the power stroke. Exhaust clears the cylinder. Multiple cylinders place those events at different points in the cycle so the crankshaft keeps turning smoothly.

Third: a propeller is a rotating airfoil. The tip covers more distance per revolution and moves faster than a section near the hub, so the blade is twisted. Blade angle is built into the geometry; blade angle of attack also depends on the relative wind created by rotation and forward airspeed.

Fourth: a fixed-pitch propeller accepts one blade-angle compromise, so rpm changes with engine torque and propeller load. A constant-speed propeller changes blade angle to maintain selected rpm within its governing range. That is why power interpretation changes from mainly tachometer-based in a fixed-pitch airplane to a manifold-pressure-and-rpm relationship in a constant-speed airplane.

Fifth: normal combustion produces a controlled pressure rise at the useful time. Detonation is explosive combustion after the intended event has begun. Preignition starts the charge before the normal spark event. Both can produce excessive heat, reduced performance, and engine damage, and both point the pilot back to proper fuel, published operating ranges, engine monitoring, and the airplane’s abnormal procedures.

**LEARNER:**

And dual magnetos add one more durable connection: each can create ignition energy independently of the airplane’s electrical system. That improves reliability in operation and means the propeller must always be treated as capable of firing the engine.

**INSTRUCTOR:**

Exactly. If you remember the power chain, the rest of the lesson has a place to attach: pressure, piston, crankshaft, propeller, thrust—and the instruments and checks that tell you whether those links are working together.
