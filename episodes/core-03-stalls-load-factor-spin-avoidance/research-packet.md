# Stalls, Load Factor, and Spin Avoidance — research packet

## Scope

- **Audience:** U.S. private-pilot airplane learners who have completed the Core 02 introduction to relative wind and angle of attack.
- **Target duration:** 30-35 minutes; approximately 5,000 spoken words after editorial expansion.
- **Core question:** How can a pilot connect angle of attack, load factor, coordination, stall cues, published speeds, and the particular airplane's POH/AFM before a stall/spin chain develops?
- **In scope:** stall as a critical-angle-of-attack condition; published one-G stall-speed context; load factor as added wing demand; recognition cues; the first aerodynamic priority in recovery; coordination; spin entry, phases, and avoidance; the base-to-final overshoot decision.
- **Out of scope:** maneuver instruction, prescribed recovery inputs, intentional-spin training, numeric load-factor calculations, aerobatics, and airplane-specific limitations.

## Likely listener traps and planned corrections

- **“The wing stalls at one airspeed.”** Define the aerodynamic condition first, then explain the one-G published value and the conditions it represents. Point listeners to the POH/AFM for their airplane's figures, configurations, limitations, and procedures.
- **“A steep or nose-high picture is the cause.”** Treat pitch picture as a clue, not the definition. The relevant angle remains between the wing's chord line and relative wind.
- **“Load factor is a number to memorize.”** Start with one G as the wing supporting one airplane-weight of aerodynamic load. In a coordinated, constant-altitude turn, tilt the wing-force arrow inward: its vertical part must still support the airplane, so the full arrow must grow. At 60 degrees of bank, the FAA's illustration is 2 G. Then distinguish this *level-turn* example from a universal bank-angle rule, and show why the resulting square-root change in stall speed is a relationship to understand—not an operating speed to invent.
- **“The stall horn is the whole warning system.”** Present warning devices, buffet, sight, sound, and feel as airplane-specific cues with limits, not as interchangeable guarantees.
- **“Save the turn with rudder.”** Use a bounded base-to-final overshoot scenario: the safe decision is to stop trying to force the runway alignment and use the appropriate alternative, not to create a skid. No invented radio call or maneuver procedure is used.
- **“Spin recovery is one universal mnemonic.”** Explain that the ACS requires knowledge of recovery, while the POH/AFM governs the airplane-specific procedure and limitations. The episode concentrates on avoiding the stalled-and-yawed entry condition.

## Drafting choices from the playbook

- Each major idea follows: define it, give a mental picture, distinguish what changes, bridge to the POH/AFM or ACS, then state the takeaway.
- The Learner asks only for an unmade connection; the Instructor establishes conditions before any Learner summary.
- The Announcer announces only the section shift in one short sentence.
- Make load factor a major teaching block: define one G and two G in plain language, use the tilted-arrow picture for a coordinated constant-altitude turn, then give one carefully bounded square-root illustration. Do not make a bank angle, a calculated speed, or a seat-of-the-pants feeling a substitute for the POH/AFM.
- Forward references are specific: Core 04 will take up flight controls and stability; later episodes will return to aircraft-specific performance and deeper load-factor applications.
