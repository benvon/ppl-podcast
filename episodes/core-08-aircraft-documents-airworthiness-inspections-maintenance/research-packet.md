# Aircraft Documents, Airworthiness, Inspections, and Maintenance — research packet

## Scope

- Audience: U.S. private-pilot airplane learners preparing for ground study,
  airplane checkout conversations, and the practical test.
- Target duration: approximately 39 minutes, supported by a complete spoken
  explanation and one applied scenario.
- Core question: How does a pilot use aircraft documents, maintenance records,
  an inspection status, and the actual condition of the airplane to decide
  whether the airplane is airworthy for a proposed flight?
- Aircraft boundary: ordinary part 91 operations in common standard-category
  airplanes. Exact equipment, limitations, supplements, inspection programs,
  and maintenance procedures remain aircraft- and operation-specific.
- Out of scope: aircraft purchase/title diligence; detailed maintenance
  procedures; experimental and special-category operating-limitations systems;
  the detailed light-sport and sport-pilot changes in MOSAIC; international
  document requirements; and a catalog of every part 43 appendix A(c) task.

## Likely trouble spots

- Misconception: “The airworthiness certificate proves the airplane is
  airworthy today.”
  - Correction: The certificate is one necessary document. Current status also
    depends on conformity, condition for safe operation, and required
    maintenance.
  - Source: PHAK Ch. 9 pp. 9-6–9-7; 14 CFR §§ 21.181 and 91.7.
- Misconception: “ARROW tells me everything I need.”
  - Correction: A mnemonic can prompt document checks, but it does not decide
    certificate currency, aircraft-specific manual requirements, inspection
    status, AD compliance, or present condition.
  - Source: 14 CFR §§ 91.9 and 91.203; PHAK Ch. 9.
- Misconception: “A POH I found online is the airplane's approved manual.”
  - Correction: Generic owner information is not necessarily approved,
    current, or aircraft-specific. Verify the manual, revisions, supplements,
    placards, and weight-and-balance/equipment data for that airplane.
  - Source: PHAK Ch. 9 pp. 9-1–9-5; 14 CFR § 91.9.
- Misconception: “Every training airplane needs a 100-hour inspection.”
  - Correction: The regulatory trigger is operation-specific. Flight
    instruction for hire triggers it when the instructor provides the aircraft.
    A private owner flying their own airplane for personal use does not trigger
    the rule merely by accumulating another 100 hours.
  - Source: 14 CFR § 91.409(b).
- Misconception: “A dead item is legal if it is not named in § 91.205.”
  - Correction: Without an MEL, the pilot must also check the VFR-day
    type-certification requirements, the aircraft equipment list and KOEL,
    other operating rules, ADs, proper disposition and placarding, and whether
    the item creates a hazard.
  - Source: 14 CFR § 91.213(d); AC 91-67A Ch. 4.
- Misconception: “INOPERATIVE tape fixes the problem.”
  - Correction: Placarding follows the required-item decision and proper
    removal or deactivation; deactivation can itself be maintenance.
  - Source: 14 CFR § 91.213(d); AC 91-67A pp. 4-1–4-2.
- Misconception: “A private pilot may perform simple work if it feels easy.”
  - Correction: The task must fit the appendix A(c) preventive-maintenance list,
    avoid complex assembly, fit the pilot/aircraft eligibility rule, and be
    performed and recorded correctly.
  - Source: 14 CFR §§ 43.3(g), 43.7(f), 43.9(a); part 43 appendix A(c).
- Misconception: “Maintenance release means the pilot has nothing left to do.”
  - Correction: Verify the return-to-service entry, inspect the work area and
    configuration, use the aircraft checklist, and plan any required or prudent
    operational check with a qualified instructor or mechanic.
  - Source: 14 CFR § 91.407; PHAK Ch. 9.

## Editorial risks to avoid

- Dense opening that explains the whole lesson before the Learner has a reason to ask.
- Long lists of similar outcomes, conditions, or controls that obscure the decision point.
- Using an undefined term as a later decision rule or safety backstop.
- Repeating disclaimer-like regulatory hedges after the required production notice.
- Repeating generic references to approved aircraft documents instead of defining the relevant POH/AFM boundary once.
- Bumper-sticker contrasts, quips, and compressed phrases that sound polished on
  the page but make the lesson harder to understand on one hearing.

## Source currency and editorial decisions

- The eCFR pages were checked August 24, 2026; the site identified the title as
  current through August 20, 2026. The source ledger records that snapshot
  rather than implying permanent currentness.
- FAA-S-ACS-6C (April 2024, effective May 31, 2024) is the current private-pilot
  airplane ACS listed by FAA at research time.
- PHAK FAA-H-8083-25C Chapter 9 is the primary teaching anchor. The October 20,
  2025 MOSAIC addendum is retained to prevent obsolete light-sport statements,
  but the lesson does not teach those special cases.
- Regulatory claims come from current eCFR section pages. PHAK and AC 91-67A
  explain the decision model; they do not replace the regulations.
- The inoperative-equipment segment first establishes whether § 91.213 provides
  a path for the planned operation, then asks the PIC to evaluate the capability
  remaining for that flight.
