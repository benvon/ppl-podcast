# Core 13 research packet: Weather Information and Preflight Decisions

## Lesson purpose

This episode teaches a private-pilot applicant to assemble individual weather reports, forecasts, advisories, and briefing resources into a route-specific decision. The central question is not merely, “Can I decode this product?” It is, “What does this evidence say about this route, at this time, and what decision does it support?”

The continuing example is an explicitly hypothetical VFR trip from Northern California to Wisconsin. No weather in the scenario is represented as current or historical. The route is a planning frame that lets the lesson revisit departure, western terrain, en route stops, the Plains, destination timing, and alternatives without inventing a real briefing.

Target length: approximately 35 to 40 minutes, or about 5,000 to 5,800 spoken words.

## Source posture

The PHAK Chapter 13 remains the primary learner anchor. The April 2026 Aviation Weather Handbook supplies current detailed product descriptions. AC 91-92 establishes briefing structure, and AIM 7-1-9 establishes cockpit-weather limitations. The ACS supplies scope and assessment outcomes. Section 91.103 supplies the regulatory starting point. Current Aviation Weather Center GFA help documents how the live graphical interface presents layers, time, altitude, and interpolated analyses.

The Airplane Flying Handbook is complementary when a weather fact needs practical flight context. This episode does not depend on a maneuver or aircraft-control claim that requires an AFH passage.

## Teaching architecture

Every product is approached through four questions:

1. Is this an observation, analysis, forecast, or advisory?
2. What geographic area and altitude does it describe?
3. When was it observed or issued, and when is it valid?
4. What planning decision can it inform, and what other evidence should confirm or challenge that conclusion?

The route worksheet organizes evidence by segment and time rather than by website or product name. The fictional planning sequence moves from an early outlook, through a standard departure-day briefing, to focused abbreviated updates. It ends by making the decision and its triggers explicit, then shows how inflight information can support a continue/divert decision.

## Important distinctions

- An application is an access tool; the METAR, TAF, advisory, or forecast is the product. A polished display does not expand the underlying product’s scope.
- A METAR is a point observation, not a route forecast. Several observations can reveal a pattern, but gaps remain evidence gaps.
- A surface analysis is an analyzed current snapshot, not a forecast.
- A TAF has terminal geographic scope. It can support a destination or stopover decision but does not describe an entire leg.
- A GFA gridded analysis interpolates between observations. Its smooth color field is not proof of actual conditions at every pixel.
- Advisory graphics summarize an area. Text, valid time, altitude, movement, and surrounding evidence remain important.
- The absence of a PIREP is not a positive report of good conditions.
- Receiving a briefing is not the same as reaching a defensible decision.
- A go decision at departure does not end weather decision-making. The plan needs update points and practical triggers for delay, route change, landing short, or diversion.

## Vocabulary to establish before use

Observation; analysis; forecast; advisory; valid time; issue time; METAR; SPECI; PIREP; surface analysis; Graphical Forecasts for Aviation or GFA; Terminal Aerodrome Forecast or TAF; AIRMET; SIGMET; Convective SIGMET; Convective Outlook; outlook briefing; standard briefing; abbreviated briefing; personal minimums; strategic weather use; tactical weather use.

## Product-to-route map

| Route question | Primary evidence | Corroborating evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can the flight depart Northern California VFR? | Departure METAR/SPECI, nearby observations, short-range forecast | GFA ceiling/visibility layers, TAF where available, terrain and alternate departure options |
| Is the western terrain corridor usable? | GFA clouds/ceiling/visibility, winds aloft, advisories | PIREPs, nearby observations, route-specific forecasts, surface pattern |
| Is a planned fuel or overnight stop usable at arrival time? | TAF where issued, destination observations as time approaches | GFA forecasts, nearby stations, advisories, alternate airports |
| Is the Plains segment exposed to convection? | Convective Outlook, Convective SIGMETs, radar and forecast layers | Surface pattern, TAFs, timing, broad alternatives outside the threatened area |
| Is Wisconsin arrival still supported? | Arrival-time TAF and forecast evidence | Current observations, nearby stations, advisories, alternate and delay options |

## Editorial and validation risks

- Avoid reading raw code strings as a substitute for teaching interpretation.
- Do not create fictional current weather values. Use conditional examples: “If the observation showed…”
- Avoid implying that one product, one briefing provider, or one app is complete by itself.
- Do not turn personal minimums into universal numbers.
- Keep the regulatory detail limited to the practical preflight obligation and ACS decision task.
- Repeat the distinction between time of issue and valid time when it matters, but do not turn the episode into a decoding catalog.
- Define the destination arrival window before using a TAF change group in a decision.

## Open source question

The ACS still names a Ceiling and Visibility Analysis. The current Aviation Weather Center presents gridded ceiling and visibility analysis within GFA. The draft describes the current presentation and retains the ACS learning objective without claiming that a legacy standalone display is the only current interface. Formal source-relevance review should scrutinize that wording.

## Source-alignment revision

After formal relevance review, the package separates the ACS resource list from its decision skills, uses the exact AC 91-92 pages for briefing types and standard briefing elements, and uses the exact Aviation Weather Handbook pages for SPECI criteria, PIREP UA/UUA report types, TAF terminal scope, mountain-wave formation and rotors, Convective SIGMETs, and the Convective Outlook description. A renewed formal source-relevance review is required because these are material spoken and claim changes.
